THREE MORE GROUNDS UNDER YOUR FEET

SOUNDS · 9/29 · dirt, sand and a wood floor, built the same way the sidewalk was
THE SIDEWALK ALREADY GOT FIXED. THESE THREE NEVER HAD A CHANCE TO. Same idea as the concrete and asphalt you already heard: a real boot hitting real ground is a hard click (a monopole radiating the change in force) plus the ground ringing its own notes plus a few dozen grains crushing. No noise generator anywhere. What changes per ground is how stiff it is and how much it damps.

Dirt and sand are the honest surprise. Packed soil and loose sand are so much softer than a sidewalk that the same formula puts their ring below what you can hear at all, so turning the ring off (rather than tuning it to a note nobody would hear) is the physically correct move, not a shortcut. What you hear on those two is almost entirely the click and the grit. The wood floor is the opposite: floorboards on joists really do ring, so it keeps almost none of its ring damped out, on purpose.
ITEM ONE · DIRT · packed trail soil

A FOOTSTEP ON DIRT

Subgrade-order stiffness, tens of megapascals, two orders of magnitude softer than concrete. The ring is real physics, not a mute button: at that softness the plate formula's own mode sits below hearing, so the loss that would kill an AUDIBLE ring on concrete kills an inaudible one here just as fast.
ITEM TWO · SAND · loose, dry, no coherent ring

A FOOTSTEP ON SAND

Not the "sand" you killed twice for sounding like band-limited noise -- that was a filter recipe standing in for every surface at once. This is one real material: loose sand does not propagate a plate wave the way a slab does, so almost the whole sound is the contact and the grain crush, the way a boot sinking into sand actually behaves.
ITEM THREE · A WOOD FLOOR · floorboards on joists

A FOOTSTEP ON A WOOD FLOOR

The one surface here that really does ring: 25 mm floorboards on joists keep almost all of their ring, on purpose, because a wood floor genuinely does that.
