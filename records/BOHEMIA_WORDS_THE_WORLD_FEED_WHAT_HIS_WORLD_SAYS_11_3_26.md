## 2. HOW A CURRENCY'S DEATH LOOKED FROM THE STREET AND FROM ABROAD (real; the texture the world posts borrow)
ECONOMY's own round on the first month (records/BOHEMIA_ECONOMY_DAY_67_THE_DOLLARS_LAST_MONTH_10_10_26.md) has the shop, wage and
rent week by week. This adds what people SAID and SAW, on the street and from far away, because a feed is that.

**ARGENTINA, 2001-2002.** The corralito (1 December 2001) capped cash withdrawals at 250 pesos a week; the street answered with the
cacerolazo, pots banged on balconies, and "que se vayan todos" (they all must go). Five presidents in about two weeks; the president
left the Casa Rosada by helicopter (20 December). Within weeks, provincial scrip (the patacón) and the trueque barter clubs, where
millions traded with their own paper "créditos". From abroad: the diaspora sending money home, and the world watching bank fronts
dented by pots. (Wikipedia, "December 2001 riots in Argentina", "Corralito", "Patacón (bond)"; IMF Independent Evaluation Office,
"The IMF and Argentina, 1991-2001", 2004.) USED: the pots, the scrip that one town takes and the next does not, the barter clubs,
news about a government arriving as a rumour of a helicopter.

**ZIMBABWE, 2007-2009.** Monthly inflation peaked near 79.6 billion percent (November 2008; Hanke and Kwok, Cato Journal 29(2),
2009); the 100 trillion dollar note (January 2009) became a souvenir sold to tourists. Shops quoted in US dollars and rand before it
was legal; people crossed to South Africa at Beitbridge for groceries; relatives abroad sent food parcels and paid bills from outside.
(Hanke and Kwok 2009; Wikipedia, "Hyperinflation in Zimbabwe".) USED: the big note as a joke, the border run for groceries, the
cousin abroad paying for things, the old money as a souvenir.

**VENEZUELA, 2016-2019.** Bolívar notes were worth less than paper: people wove bags and wallets out of them to sell (Reuters, 2018);
about seven million left the country (UNHCR and the R4V platform, 2023), many walking across the Simón Bolívar bridge into Cúcuta;
the ones who left sent remittances and voice notes; at home, queues, price controls, and the "bachaqueros" reselling controlled goods.
(UNHCR, "Venezuela situation"; R4V, Refugees and Migrants from Venezuela, figures 2023.) USED: crafts made of dead money, the walk
across a bridge, the family split across countries, the voice note from the one who left.

**LEBANON, 2019-2023.** The lira lost over 95 percent of its value; banks froze dollar deposits informally (the trapped dollar got a
name, the "lollar"); the World Bank called it likely one of the three worst crises since the mid-1800s (Lebanon Economic Monitor,
Spring 2021); depositors held up their own banks to take their own savings (September 2022, one for a sister's cancer treatment);
private generator owners became the power company. (World Bank LEM Spring 2021; Reuters and AP coverage, September 2022.) USED: a
nickname for money that exists on paper only, the neighbourhood generator man, holding up a bank for your own savings, the diaspora
flying in with cash in a suitcase.

**THE SOVIET COLLAPSE, 1991-1992.** Prices freed on 2 January 1992 and rose about 2,500 percent that year; pensioners sold their
belongings outside metro stations; "shuttle traders" (chelnoki) carried goods from Turkey and China in checked bags; factories paid
wages in their own product (in the late 1990s arrears, workers paid in bras, coffins, vodka); abroad, the West watched queues on
television and flew in aid (Operation Provide Hope, 1992). (Wikipedia, "Economy of Russia" 1992 price liberalisation; Gaddy and Ickes,
"Russia's Virtual Economy", Brookings 2002, on barter and payment in kind.) USED: pay in the factory's own product, the shuttle trader,
the selling of a life on a blanket, the strange aid flight.

**WHAT ALL FIVE SAY, AND IT DRIVES ROUND 2:** (1) news of a collapse travels as FAMILY, not headlines: a cousin's message, a remittance,
a voice note; (2) everybody prices their day in whatever is hard (dollars, rand, batteries); (3) a dead currency becomes a craft and a
joke; (4) a government's fall arrives as a rumour of one detail (a helicopter); (5) the people who left talk to the people who stayed,
and each thinks the other got it worse.
