## 1. WHAT THE STUDIES SAY MAKES A PLAYER CARE IN THE FIRST MINUTES

Ten findings. Each is a study or a measured number, then what it means for us in one line. The web was
read through search extracts only (this box cannot open most pages); where only the abstract was seen,
the line says so. Sources are numbered at the bottom.

**S1. THREE NEEDS PREDICT FUN AND WHETHER PEOPLE KEEP PLAYING: CHOICE, GETTING GOOD, PEOPLE.** Ryan, Rigby
and Przybylski (2006, four studies, the last one an online community) measured that a player's feeling of
autonomy (my choices matter), competence (I am getting it right) and relatedness (I am connected to
someone) each predicted enjoyment, and in the fourth study each predicted who kept playing [1]. The 2010
follow-up made it the model of engagement [1]. (Abstract-level; sample sizes not read.)
FOR US: the first minutes owe him all three: a choice that changes what happens, a small thing done
right, and a face that knows him.

**S2. A TUTORIAL ONLY PAYS IN A COMPLEX GAME, AND THERE IT PAYS A LOT.** Andersen and others (CHI 2012)
A/B tested eight tutorial designs on 45,000 players across three games: in the complex one (Foldit)
tutorials raised play time up to 29% and progress up to 75%; in the two simple ones they changed nothing
measurable [2].
FOR US: Battle Brothers is a complex game and so are we. Teaching is not optional, but it is cheap only
when it rides the play (TUNING 10/23 change 8, one voice teaches the first hour).

**S3. THE FIRST GLANCE DECIDES, AND IT COLOURS EVERYTHING AFTER.** Lindgaard and others (2006, three
studies) showed pages for 50 milliseconds; the ratings matched the ratings people gave after looking
longer, and the authors measured a halo: the first feeling carried over to how usable and trustworthy
people judged the rest [3]. (Websites, not games; the halo is the part that transfers.)
FOR US: the first frame after BEGIN is judged in a blink, and it is the same frame every new game (section 4).

**S4. CURIOSITY NEEDS A GAP OF THE RIGHT SIZE.** Loewenstein (1994) framed curiosity as a felt gap in what
you know; later work measured it peaking at a MIDDLE gap: knowing nothing gives you nothing to wonder
about, knowing everything gives you nothing to chase [4].
FOR US: one open question about THIS world in the first minute (who did this, where did they go), never
a lore dump (gap too small) and never silence (gap too big to see).

**S5. YOU LOVE WHAT YOU MADE, BUT ONLY IF YOU FINISHED IT.** Norton, Mochon and Ariely (2012, four studies:
IKEA boxes, origami, Lego) measured that people value what they built about as high as an expert's work,
and the effect VANISHED when the thing was left unfinished or destroyed [5].
FOR US: the first five minutes should hand him one thing he finished himself (a fight won, a job paid, a
name he typed), and the run's things should be HIS, not picked off a list.

**S6. MAKING YOUR OWN CHARACTER RAISES HOW MUCH YOU IDENTIFY, AND THAT RAISES FUN.** Customising raised
identification in a League of Legends lab test (Bohffel 2022, 24 people), in a VR exercise game (Koulouris
2020, with intrinsic motivation too) and over ten hours of Lord of the Rings Online (66 people, both time
and customising raised it); identification went with fun and felt competence [6]. Small lab samples, all
pointing one way.
FOR US: the three people he starts with should be shown as people (faces, names, one line each) at the
door, not as a count.

**S7. MOST OF THE PLAYERS A GAME LOSES, IT LOSES IN THE FIRST HALF HOUR.** Day-one return on phones: about 27%
average in 2025 (Adjust, via Segwise), top quarter just over 30%, top tenth about 40% (GameAnalytics 2026,
16,262 titles) [7]. Steam's achievement counts (owners as the denominator, so they overstate): Starfield's
first trip to space, about 30 minutes in, about 75%; Fallout 4's first step out of the vault 81%; Skyrim's
first dungeon 83%; Resident Evil 7 had about 9% who never got past the opening cutscene [8].
FOR US: one player in four or five is gone before the first half hour ends, in big-budget games. A stranger
decides inside our demo's five minutes.

**S8. PEOPLE SKIP TALK THAT DOES NOT MATTER.** BioWare's telemetry: Mass Effect 2 players skipped about 15%
of conversations, mostly the low-stakes ones in hubs (2010) [9]; Kojima said many players skipped Death
Stranding's cutscenes (no number) [9].
FOR US: an opening text must be short, skippable and about HIM; the second votes already rule nothing is
forced.

**S9. A WORLD FEELS NEW BY WHAT YOU NOTICE, NOT BY WHAT THE MATHS CHANGED.** Kate Compton's "10,000 bowls of
oatmeal": a generator can make 10,000 mathematically different bowls a player sees as the same bowl;
PERCEPTUAL uniqueness is the real measure, "and it's darn tough" [10]. On Spore she kept a hand-picked list
of good seeds and threw away the dull ones [10]. Emily Short: variety that touches no mechanic gets ignored
once the player sees through it [10].
FOR US: changing the seed underneath is worthless if the first screen, the first town, the first two jobs and
the first posts are the same. Change what he SEES and what he DOES first.

**S10. A STORY MADE BY PLAY BEATS A STORY TOLD.** Shadow of Mordor's Nemesis: the orc that killed you is
promoted, remembers you and taunts you, and the rematch is personal [11]. No study measured it; player
accounts say a nameless enemy became someone once it had a name and a grudge, and a minority found it no
different from any captain [11].
FOR US: the first named enemy should come out of HIS first minutes (the man who ran, the crew that robbed
the convoy), never a fixed villain. The Battle Brothers way, too: no authored first fight (the fifth votes).

ALSO ON FILE (TUNING 10/23, cited, not re-searched): time to fun under 60 seconds on phones (Jon Lai), the
basics inside 60 seconds (Rohrl, PopCap); Battle Brothers' own developer said new players meet "really
strong enemies at the very beginning, then [get] wrecked".
