## 1. WHAT THE STUDIES SAY MAKES A PLAYER CARE IN THE FIRST MINUTES

Ten findings. Each is a study or a measured number, then what it means for us in one line. The web was
read through search extracts only (this box cannot open most pages); where only the abstract was seen,
the line says so. Sources are numbered at the bottom.

**S1. THREE NEEDS PREDICT FUN AND WHETHER PEOPLE KEEP PLAYING: CHOICE, GETTING GOOD, PEOPLE.** Ryan, Rigby
and Przybylski (2006, four studies, the last one an online community) measured that a player's feeling of
autonomy (my choices matter), competence (I am getting it right) and relatedness (I am connected to
someone) each predicted enjoyment, and in the fourth study each predicted who kept playing [1]. The 2010
follow-up made it the model of engagement [1]. (Abstract-level; sample sizes not read.)
FOR US: the first minutes owe him all three: a choice that changes what happens, a small thing done
right, and a face that knows him.

**S2. A TUTORIAL ONLY PAYS IN A COMPLEX GAME, AND THERE IT PAYS A LOT.** Andersen and others (CHI 2012)
A/B tested eight tutorial designs on 45,000 players across three games: in the complex one (Foldit)
tutorials raised play time up to 29% and progress up to 75%; in the two simple ones they changed nothing
measurable [2].
FOR US: Battle Brothers is a complex game and so are we. Teaching is not optional, but it is cheap only
when it rides the play (TUNING 10/23 change 8, one voice teaches the first hour).

**S3. THE FIRST GLANCE DECIDES, AND IT COLOURS EVERYTHING AFTER.** Lindgaard and others (2006, three
studies) showed pages for 50 milliseconds; the ratings matched the ratings people gave after looking
longer, and the authors measured a halo: the first feeling carried over to how usable and trustworthy
people judged the rest [3]. (Websites, not games; the halo is the part that transfers.)
FOR US: the first frame after BEGIN is judged in a blink, and it is the same frame every new game (section 4).

**S4. CURIOSITY NEEDS A GAP OF THE RIGHT SIZE.** Loewenstein (1994) framed curiosity as a felt gap in what
you know; later work measured it peaking at a MIDDLE gap: knowing nothing gives you nothing to wonder
about, knowing everything gives you nothing to chase [4].
FOR US: one open question about THIS world in the first minute (who did this, where did they go), never
a lore dump (gap too small) and never silence (gap too big to see).

**S5. YOU LOVE WHAT YOU MADE, BUT ONLY IF YOU FINISHED IT.** Norton, Mochon and Ariely (2012, four studies:
IKEA boxes, origami, Lego) measured that people value what they built about as high as an expert's work,
and the effect VANISHED when the thing was left unfinished or destroyed [5].
FOR US: the first five minutes should hand him one thing he finished himself (a fight won, a job paid, a
name he typed), and the run's things should be HIS, not picked off a list.

**S6. MAKING YOUR OWN CHARACTER RAISES HOW MUCH YOU IDENTIFY, AND THAT RAISES FUN.** Customising raised
identification in a League of Legends lab test (Boffel 2022, 24 people), in a VR exercise game (Koulouris
2020, with intrinsic motivation too) and over ten hours of Lord of the Rings Online (66 people, both time
and customising raised it); identification went with fun and felt competence [6]. Small lab samples, all
pointing one way.
FOR US: the three people he starts with should be shown as people (faces, names, one line each) at the
door, not as a count.

**S7. MOST OF THE PLAYERS A GAME LOSES, IT LOSES IN THE FIRST HALF HOUR.** Day-one return on phones: about 27%
average in 2025 (Adjust, via Segwise), top quarter just over 30%, top tenth about 40% (GameAnalytics 2026,
16,262 titles) [7]. Steam's achievement counts (owners as the denominator, so they overstate): Starfield's
first trip to space, about 30 minutes in, about 75%; Fallout 4's first step out of the vault 81%; Skyrim's
first dungeon 83%; Resident Evil 7 had about 9% who never got past the opening cutscene [8].
FOR US: one player in four or five is gone before the first half hour ends, in big-budget games. A stranger
decides inside our demo's five minutes.

**S8. PEOPLE SKIP TALK THAT DOES NOT MATTER.** BioWare's telemetry: Mass Effect 2 players skipped about 15%
of conversations, mostly the low-stakes ones in hubs (2010) [9]; Kojima said many players skipped Death
Stranding's cutscenes (no number) [9].
FOR US: an opening text must be short, skippable and about HIM; the second votes already rule nothing is
forced.

**S9. A WORLD FEELS NEW BY WHAT YOU NOTICE, NOT BY WHAT THE MATHS CHANGED.** Kate Compton's "10,000 bowls of
oatmeal": a generator can make 10,000 mathematically different bowls a player sees as the same bowl;
PERCEPTUAL uniqueness is the real measure, "and it's darn tough" [10]. On Spore she kept a hand-picked list
of good seeds and threw away the dull ones [10]. Emily Short: variety that touches no mechanic gets ignored
once the player sees through it [10].
FOR US: changing the seed underneath is worthless if the first screen, the first town, the first two jobs and
the first posts are the same. Change what he SEES and what he DOES first.

**S10. A STORY MADE BY PLAY BEATS A STORY TOLD.** Shadow of Mordor's Nemesis: the orc that killed you is
promoted, remembers you and taunts you, and the rematch is personal [11]. No study measured it; player
accounts say a nameless enemy became someone once it had a name and a grudge, and a minority found it no
different from any captain [11].
FOR US: the first named enemy should come out of HIS first minutes (the man who ran, the crew that robbed
the convoy), never a fixed villain. The Battle Brothers way, too: no authored first fight (the fifth votes).

ALSO ON FILE (TUNING 10/23, cited, not re-searched): time to fun under 60 seconds on phones (Jon Lai), the
basics inside 60 seconds (Rohrl, PopCap); Battle Brothers' own developer said new players meet "really
strong enemies at the very beginning, then [get] wrecked".

### BATTLE BROTHERS FIRST (how the system works, rule 41)

- THE SCREENS (recall, the order checked against the wiki's pages): (1) pick one of 15 origins, each a
  picture, two or three sentences and a difficulty mark; (2) the company's name and a banner; (3) combat

  About 3 screens and 5 to 10 taps, under a minute for someone who knows what he wants.
- THE WORLD IS NEW EVERY CAMPAIGN, AND THAT IS STATED AS A RULE (wiki, Game Guide): "Generated map layouts are
  stored in map seeds. Starting a new campaign with a same map seed will always generate the same map
  including locations and their named items." Coastline, rivers, roads, forests, hills, swamps, snow in the
  north, desert south; villages with their attached farms and mines; enemy camps that grow (library
  01_WORLDMAP). And the POWERS are new: "Like most things in Battle Brothers, noble houses are procedurally
  generated for each new campaign... Each noble house comes with a unique coat of arms, description and set
  of procedurally generated leaders" (wiki, Factions and Relations, the developers' words).
- THE OPENING IS ONE TEXT BOX FROM THE ORIGIN (wiki, every origin page and Dev Blog 113): "Each of those comes
  with a flavor introduction, different starting characters, equipment, resources, and special rules for
  your campaign." The introduction is the same event window every Battle Brothers event uses: one painted
  picture, one paragraph, one button. The paragraph names WHO YOU ARE, WHAT YOU LOST OR WANT, and A
  DIRECTION. Their own words, by origin:
  - A New Company (easy): "After years of bloodying your sword for meager pay, you've saved enough crowns to
    start your very own mercenary company." Tagged "Impatient: a quick start into the world".
  - Rebuilding a Company (the tutorial start): "You are second-in-command in a mercenary company that has been
    tracking a brigand named Hoggart for some time now. An unexpected turn of events leaves the company in
    shatters, and you in charge." A NAMED ENEMY, A LOSS AND A GOAL in two sentences, then a guided first fight
    and an optional hunt for Hoggart (wiki: "Starts off with a basic tutorial; can choose to do the Hoggart
    mission").
  - Lone Wolf (hard): "A hedge knight tall as a tree, you never needed anybody for long. Is it true still?"
    A QUESTION, not an instruction.
  - Northern Raiders: "if your potential employers are willing to forgive your past transgressions"; two of
    three noble houses start hostile (search, Dev Blog 114). THE ORIGIN CHANGES HOW THE WORLD MEETS YOU.
  - Southern Mercenaries: "A quick start into the SOUTHERN PART OF THE WORLD". THE ORIGIN CHANGES WHERE YOU
    START.
  - Peasant Militia: "a home village that idolizes you and gives you great prices" (Dev Blog 113). THE ORIGIN
    GIVES YOU A HOME ON THE MAP.
- THE SPINE IS CHOSEN, NOT GIVEN (wiki, Ambitions): every few days the company picks one goal from a short
  list ("hire 12 men", "amass 5,000 crowns"); each pays at least 100 renown and lifts the mood; cancelling
  costs morale. A goal he picked is a goal he owns (S5).
- NO AUTHORED FIRST FIGHT outside the tutorial origin; the map is paused under the text box; the first
  contract is found, not handed over (the fifth votes keep this).

### CUTSCENE, TEXT, OR STRAIGHT IN: WHAT THE TABLE SAYS

- THE LONG CUTSCENE LOSES PEOPLE BEFORE THE GAME STARTS: about 9% of Resident Evil 7 players never got past
  its opening cutscene (src 8, a trophy proxy). Players skip low-stakes talk (S8). None of the run-based
  strategy games above opens with a long film.
- THE TEXT BOX FROM THE ORIGIN IS THE GENRE'S STANDARD: Battle Brothers, FTL and RimWorld all open on ONE
  screen of 25 to 60 words that names who you are, what you lost or want, and where to go, then hands over
  the map. The good ones end on a question or a threat, not an instruction (the Lone Wolf's "Is it true
  still?", FTL's fleet behind you).
- STRAIGHT IN WITH A VOICE OVER PLAY (Hades, Darkest Dungeon) is the strongest when there is a voice worth
  hearing.
- FOR US: our rules already choose. Text comes from a mouth with a portrait and nothing pops up (rule 19);
  nothing is forced and nothing speaks in the first 60 s of play (the second votes). So the Battle Brothers
  text box becomes THE ORIGIN SPOKEN BY ITS OWN FACE AT THE DOOR (before play, while he picks), and the map
  opens on a place that is already telling the story (section 3). The first spoken line on the map waits past
  60 s and is a person standing there, never a stop.

## 3. WHAT MAKES A WORLD FEEL GENERATED AT THE FIRST LOOK

Compton's rule decides this section (S9): only what he NOTICES counts. Ranked by how fast he notices it.

1. **WHERE HE STANDS.** The loudest signal and the first one. Kenshi's start IS a place (the Hub, a random
   waystation, rock bottom); Project Zomboid keeps one real map and moves the town and the house; Battle
   Brothers sends the Southern Mercenaries south and gives the Militia a home village; RimWorld lets him pick
   the spot on a whole planet. If the first glance is the same corner, every other change underneath reads
   as the same game (S3: the first glance colours the rest).
2. **WHAT IS NEXT TO HIM.** The nearest town, who holds it, and the two jobs it posts. Battle Brothers' three
   noble houses are new each campaign "with a unique coat of arms, description and set of procedurally
   generated leaders" (wiki). Ours: the same Church two blocks west, every time.
3. **SOMETHING HAPPENED HERE BEFORE HE CAME.** Dwarf Fortress runs years of history before he settles; Caves
   of Qud writes five sultans with 10 to 22 events each, new every world (src 19). A world feels generated
   when it has a PAST he did not see happen: a burned seat, a convoy in a ditch, a dry standpipe with a
   queue. One such sight, at his corner, from this world's own state, is worth more than a re-rolled street grid.
4. **NAMES HE HAS NOT HEARD BEFORE.** New leaders, new faces, his own crew's names (RimWorld rolls three
   colonists with a name, a childhood and an adult past; Shadows of Doubt names every citizen). Names are
   cheap to roll and are read in the first minute.
5. **THE WORLD'S STATE.** Who is at war with whom on day one, what water costs, which outfit holds the
   fortress. Our posts already read the world's state ("5 outfits holding a fortress between them"), so a
   new world would already post new lines.
6. **THE SHAPE OF THE GROUND.** Coastlines, rivers, the Strip's side of centre. Real but slowest to notice:
   he sees it on the zoomed-out map, not in the first ten seconds. It still matters on the second and third
   game, when he starts to compare.
7. **A NAME FOR THE WORLD.** Minecraft and Spelunky show the seed as a short string he can say and hand to a
   friend. A valley with a name ("SIN CITY", our engine's own example) is a valley that is HIS.

TWO GUARDS:
- **KEEP WHAT MAKES IT VEGAS.** Caves of Qud keeps a few places fixed (Grit Gate) and rolls the rest; that is
  our mix. His 7/5 ruling already keeps the Strip sacred, with the dam south, the mountains on the rim and
  the solar field north (laws/BOHEMIA_ADDENDUM_VEGAS_GEOGRAPHY_7_4_26.md). Las Vegas is the city; the
  valley rolls around its landmarks.
- **CURATE BEFORE YOU TRUST THE DICE.** Compton kept a hand-picked list of good Spore seeds and dropped the
  bad ones because it was faster than a better algorithm (src 10). A seed that puts the start four blocks from
  a dire-wolf den is a different game, not a fresh one. Every start must pass a short test (section 5, change 1).

THE TEST THAT SAYS IT WORKED (for EYES, by eye, and for PLUMBER, by machine): two new games side by side on
the same origin. A stranger names three differences in the first ten seconds. The first screens differ by
more than half their pixels once the walkers are masked (today 2.1%, all of it walkers). Across the fifteen
origins the start corner differs in at least 13. Across ten seeds the first two jobs differ in at least nine.

### THE FINDING THAT PROVES US WRONG
