# WORDS Q9 -- WHAT A FORMER TRADE SOUNDS LIKE
# VAMILY research round, 9/5/26, lane WORDS (words-8dqrnq). MODE: RESEARCH.
# The question, verbatim: "What a former trade sounds like. A dealer, a lineman, a
# laundry chief, a pit boss: the vocabulary and metaphors a job leaves in a mouth
# (day 8's background as identity). Test lines for six trades."

## THE ANSWER IN ONE LINE
**A trade does not leave VOCABULARY in a mouth, it leaves a SOURCE DOMAIN: a way
of explaining things that are not the job. We have zero of that, and four of our
six trades never use a single word from their own trade either.**

## 1. THE FINDING THAT PROVES US WRONG
Tight test: words that belong to ONE trade and to no everyday register.

    trade      lines   uses its OWN trade words   OTHER speakers using them
    lineman       20            3                          2
    fitter        10            3                          7
    scav         100            0                          3
    trader        16            0                          9
    medic         14            0                          0
    busker        10            0                          0

**Four of six trades never say a single word from their own trade.** A medic who
never says pulse, stitch, fever, wound or dose. A busker who never says chord,
verse, tune or string. And in two cases **other speakers use that trade's
vocabulary more than the tradesperson does**: nine other people use trade-and-barter
words while the trader uses none, and three use scavenger words while the scav,
across a hundred lines, uses none.

**The trade is a label on the speaker, not a fact about the mouth.**

## 2. AND THE DEEPER TEST IS WORSE, WHICH IS WHERE THE REAL CRAFT IS
Lakoff and Johnson: a conceptual metaphor maps a SOURCE domain you know in your
body onto a TARGET domain you do not. The concrete, lived, physical domain of a
person's work is exactly the kind of source domain that gets mapped onto
everything else. **That is what a job actually leaves behind: not a glossary, a
lens.**

So the real test is not "does the lineman say cable", it is "does the lineman
explain a MARRIAGE in terms of load".

    lines carrying BOTH a trade word and a person word:  4 of 1,669

And reading all four: **not one is a metaphor.** A man in an office. Carrying pipe
for somebody. People being cool near a compressor. Every one is literal.

**NOBODY IN THIS BUILD USES THEIR TRADE AS A WAY OF SEEING ANYTHING ELSE.** Zero.
That is the whole gap, and it is a much better gap than the vocabulary one because
it is cheaper to fill and it cannot be faked by sprinkling jargon.

## 3. THE ONE THAT WORKS, AND WHY IT ONLY HALF COUNTS
The fitter's speech is genuinely trade-voiced:

    "A main keeps muck out by PUSHING, that's all it does. There's no valve in the
     world doing that job, it's just pressure, all day, every day."
    "Take the pressure off and every place somebody's joined something to that pipe
     stops being an outlet and becomes an inlet."

That is a tradesman explaining his trade correctly, which is ACCURACY. It becomes
CHARACTERISATION the moment he uses the same sentence about a person, and he never
does. The quest around him does the metaphor at the design level; his mouth does
not.

## 4. WHAT THE BEST GAMES DO
**Pentiment** is the cleanest model and it matches this question exactly. Andreas
picks where he studied, a hobby and a university subject, and those choices unlock
dialogue that leverages the chosen talent: the Craftsman reads the ART and the
structures, the Medicine student reads the BODY. **The background is not flavour
text, it is a set of things this person can SEE that others cannot.**

**Disco Elysium** runs the same idea through skills that speak as voices, so the
lens is literally a character in the room.

**The rule both land on: a background is a PERCEPTION, not a vocabulary.** What the
trade earns you is a line nobody else in the scene could have said, about something
that is not your job.

## 5. THE SIX TRADES, AS A SPEC
Each gets a source domain, not a word list. The domain is what they explain OTHER
things with.

    TRADE          SOURCE DOMAIN                 WHAT THEY SEE THAT NOBODY ELSE DOES
    dealer         odds, the house, the long run  who is due to lose and does not know it
    lineman        load, draw, what is upstream   who is quietly taking from whom
    laundry chief  what stains, what comes out    what a person did, from what is on them
    pit boss       the count, the watched room    who is being watched and by whom
    fitter         pressure, flow, backflow       which way trouble will run when the push stops
    medic          bleeding, pulse, what closes   how long a thing has left before it goes bad

**AND THE HARD RULE: the trade word must land on a NON-trade target.** A lineman
saying "warm cable" about a cable is a technician. A lineman saying "that family
has been drawing more than the line can carry for years" is a character.

## 6. WHAT THIS SAYS TO DO, WHEN THE LANE RETURNS TO BUILD
1. **ONE CROSS-DOMAIN LINE PER NAMED TRADE.** Not a glossary pass. One sentence
   each where the job explains something that is not the job. Six lines total, and
   it is the highest characterisation-per-word in any round so far.
2. **FIX THE FOUR SILENT TRADES FIRST.** The medic, the busker, the scav and the
   trader currently say nothing from their own world at all.
3. **AND THE BAN LIST FROM Q1 APPLIES HERE UNCHANGED**, because a source domain is
   also a set of things somebody would NEVER reach for: the medic does not describe
   a building in money, the dealer does not describe a person in weather.
4. **THIS IS THE CHEAPEST ITEM IN THE WHOLE QUEUE.** Six lines, and it fixes both
   the vocabulary hole and the perception hole at once.
