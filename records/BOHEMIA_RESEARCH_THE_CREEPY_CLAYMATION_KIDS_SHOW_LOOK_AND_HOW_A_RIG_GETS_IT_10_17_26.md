## PART 1: WHICH SHOWS HE MOST LIKELY MEANS

The phrase is "HBO early kids show", "creepy", "claymation". Three facts narrow it fast.

1. HBO's own children's output in the late 1990s was small: HBO Family launched its first original shows in 1999, and
   two of them were CRASHBOX and A LITTLE CURIOUS (hbo-family.fandom.com/wiki/Crashbox; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HBO_Kids).
2. Only ONE HBO kids show of that era was built mainly from stop-motion and clay: CRASHBOX. A LITTLE CURIOUS mixed clay
   and stop-motion segments with everything else. The rest of the HBO shelf (Happily Ever After, Storybook Musicals,
   Encyclopedia) was 2D cel or live action, so it cannot be the clay memory even if it sits in the same childhood.
3. One studio made the clay on HBO Family: CUPPA COFFEE STUDIOS of Toronto, under Adam Shaheen. It animated Crashbox,
   it made the HBO FAMILY 411 claymation interstitials that ran between shows on the same channel, and it later worked
   on MTV's CELEBRITY DEATHMATCH (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuppa_Coffee_Studios; hbo-family.fandom.com/wiki/Cuppa_Coffee_Studios;
   awn.com/news/cuppa-coffee-expands-studio, 2002). So "the HBO claymation vibe" is very possibly ONE STUDIO'S HAND,
   which is good news for a rig: a single consistent recipe, not a genre average.

### TIER A: HBO and HBO Family, stop-motion or clay, remembered as eerie (the strong candidates)

**CRASHBOX** (HBO Family, premiered February 1, 1999; last new episode April 1, 2000; 52 episodes by most counts,
39 by TV Tropes). Created by Eamon Harrington and John Watkin at Planet Grande Pictures; animated by Cuppa Coffee
Studios, Toronto. A kids' game show set inside a steampunk machine in a computer, cut into short puzzle segments.
Technique: Wikipedia lists "traditional 2D, stop motion, Claymation, and cutout animation" across the segments
(en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crashbox). Shaheen called it "a non-traditional game show for kids from eight to 12" with a
"British sensibility" (playbackonline.ca/2000/06/12/29418-20000612/, 2000). Known segments and their hosts: Captain
Bones, a skeleton pirate who builds equations out of his own bones; Distraction News, a cardboard-cutout anchorwoman
(Dora Smarmy) over whose broadcast distracting pictures flash; Ear We Are, two disembodied ears; Psycho Math, an
eccentric robot professor; Haunted House Party, hosted by Death; plus Revolting Slob, Dirty Pictures, Lens McCracken,
Poop or Scoop, Riddle Snake, Sketch Pad, Ten Seconds, Think Tank, Verb Stretch, Word Shake (crashbox.fandom.com/wiki/Segments;
rabbitearsblog.neocities.org/crashboxsegments). Why it reads creepy: the fan wiki says it is "remembered in due part
because of how unsettling off-the-wall the show can be" (hbo-family.fandom.com/wiki/Crashbox); a 2020s student
retrospective calls it "honestly eerie, yet unique ... because it is entirely stop-motion" and says the memory "had
haunted me for years" (mvcurrent.com/11002/features/crashbox-the-tv-show-i-honestly-forgot-existed/). The ingredients:
a skeleton, Death, severed ears and a robot as hosts; cardboard cutouts moving next to clay; hard, dark, industrial
lighting; a mechanical factory frame; stepped movement. It ran in reruns on HBO Family until the kids' block closed in
2025, which is why a 1999 child could keep meeting it for twenty years (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crashbox). THE FIRST
CANDIDATE.

**A LITTLE CURIOUS** (HBO Family, February 1, 1999 to May 1, 2000 per Wikipedia; some sources say 1998; 43 episodes
aired 1999 to 2003). Produced by Curious Pictures, New York (creator credit Steve Oakes). Each 24-minute episode is an
anthology of shorts around one word ("slippery", "up, down"). Characters are household objects: Bob the Ball, Mop,
Mr. String, Doris the Door, Little Cup. Technique, in Wikipedia's words: "stop-motion, collage animation, clay
animation, traditional 3-D and 2-D hand-drawn animation, and 3-D CGI, along with live-action segments"
(en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Little_Curious; lostmediawiki.com/A_Little_Curious_(found_HBO_Family_animated_series;_1999-2000)).
Why it reads creepy: talking household objects with faces, photographed real-world textures cut together with clay and
CG at different realism levels in the same minute (see Part 2 on realism inconsistency), and a preschool pace. No
source found calls it creepy in those words; the uncanny reading is this file's inference from its technique list, and
it is marked as such. THE SECOND CANDIDATE, and the one most likely to be tangled with Crashbox in memory since they
ran back to back on the same channel.

**HBO FAMILY 411** (HBO Family interstitials, about 1999 to the early 2000s). Short claymation shorts by Cuppa Coffee
that ran between programs; only four have resurfaced (hbo-family.fandom.com/wiki/HBO_Family_411;
hbo-family.fandom.com/wiki/Cuppa_Coffee_Studios). Nobody remembers the name; everybody who watched the channel saw them.
If he remembers "the HBO claymation" as a texture rather than a show, this is likely part of it. THE THIRD CANDIDATE,
by the same hands as the first.

### TIER B: HBO kids shows of the era that were NOT stop-motion (listed so nobody chases them)

- **HAPPILY EVER AFTER: FAIRY TALES FOR EVERY CHILD** (HBO, March 1995 to 2000, 39 episodes; narrated by Robert
  Guillaume). 2D cel animation, retold fairy tales. Not clay (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Happily_Ever_After:_Fairy_Tales_for_Every_Child).
- **ENCYCLOPEDIA** (HBO, September 1988 to February 1989, 23 episodes; HBO with Children's Television Workshop's
  Distinguished Productions; director Joshua White). Live-action sketch comedy and songs, one letter per episode. Not
  clay (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopedia_(TV_series)).
- **HBO STORYBOOK MUSICALS** (1987 to 1993). 2D animation true to the storybook illustrations. Not clay
  (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HBO_Storybook_Musicals).
- **LIFESTORIES: FAMILIES IN CRISIS** (1992 to 1996). Live action. Not clay (hbo-archives.fandom.com/wiki/List_of_Shows).

### TIER C: not HBO, but on the same shelf in a 1990s kid's memory, and the same craft

- **PEE-WEE'S PLAYHOUSE** (CBS, 1986 to 1990). Its recurring "Penny" shorts were down-shooter clay animation by
  AARDMAN (Nick Park and Richard Goleszowski on the first nine per one account, Peter Lord and David Sproxton per
  another; Craig Bartlett from season two), inside a live-action show that also used puppets, chroma key and stock
  footage (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pee-wee's_Playhouse; cartoonresearch.com/index.php/animation-anecdotes-377/;
  animationmagazine.net, 2023). The creepy ingredient is the MIX: a live man, a talking chair, clay children, all in
  one room. Often mis-filed as HBO in memory.
- **KABLAM!** (Nickelodeon, 1996 to 2000). Stop-motion segments PROMETHEUS AND BOB (Cote Zellers, 41 segments), ACTION
  LEAGUE NOW! (action figures) and LIFE WITH LOOPY. The original Prometheus and Bob pilot was never aired, reportedly
  because of its dark tone (lostmediawiki.com/Prometheus_and_Bob_...; kablam.fandom.com/wiki/Prometheus_and_Bob;
  avclub.com, 2019).
- **PINWHEEL** (Nickelodeon, 1979 to about 1990). Imported European stop-motion shorts between the puppet segments:
  CHAPI CHAPO (France, 1974, 60 five-minute episodes, two children in oversized hats), HATTYTOWN TALES (UK, 1969)
  (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapi_Chapo; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hattytown_Tales; nickelodeon.fandom.com/wiki/Pinwheel).
  Cheerful on paper; one reviewer's four-year-old cried at a Chapi Chapo ending (imdb.com/title/tt0224947/reviews/).
- **BUMP IN THE NIGHT** (ABC, 1994 to 1995, 26 or 27 episodes). Danger Productions and Greengrass; stop-motion with
  clay; Mr. Bumpy, a green monster with red snail eyes under a boy's bed (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bump_in_the_Night_(TV_series)).
- **CELEBRITY DEATHMATCH** (MTV, pilots January 1998, series May 1998 to October 2002, 75 episodes, revived 2006).
  Created by Eric Fogel; Cuppa Coffee worked on it (mentalfloss.com/entertainment/tv/celebrity-deathmatch-mtv-history;
  en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Fogel; awn.com/news/cuppa-coffee-expands-studio, 2002). The adult cousin of Crashbox, by
  some of the same hands: wet clay gore, visible thumbprints, hard arena light.
- **THE TRAP DOOR** (ITV, 1984; second series 1990; 40 episodes). Terry Brain and Charlie Mills, CMTB Animation. Berk,
  a blue blob, serves an unseen "Thing Upstairs" in a castle; each episode "blends humour with a touch of horror", with
  monsters "ranging from goofy to downright unsettling" (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Trap_Door; televisionheaven.co.uk/reviews/the-trap-door).
  The British clay-horror-comedy template that Shaheen's "British sensibility" line points at.
- **PINGU** (Switzerland, 1986 to 1993 or 2006 by source). Otmar Gutmann. Clay penguins, gibberish speech
  (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_stop_motion_artists).
- **GUMBY** (1956 to 1968; revival 1988). Art Clokey. The 1990s revival is described as "sometimes charming, sometimes
  vaguely unsettling" (go2tutors.com/strangest-kids-shows-from-the-90s/).
- **RANKIN/BASS "ANIMAGIC"** (Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer, NBC, December 6, 1964). Stop-motion shot at MOM
  Productions in Tokyo under Tadahito Mochinaga; puppets articulated at every joint, bodies reused under different heads,
  sprayed with an anti-reflection coating (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolph_the_Red-Nosed_Reindeer_(TV_special);
  remindmagazine.com/article/2403/; pbs.org/wgbh/roadshow/appraisals/rankinbass-the-daydreamer-puppets-ca-1966/).
  Matte, chalky surfaces, big heads on modular bodies, 18 months for half an hour.
- **WILL VINTON** coined and trademarked "Claymation" in 1978; Closed Mondays won the 1974 Oscar; the trademark left
  his hands with the studio by 2003 (oregonencyclopedia.org/articles/vinton_william_t._will/; cartoonbrew.com, 2018).

### TIER D: the lineage the craft comes from (for the bible, not for the memory)

- **GEORGE PAL'S PUPPETOONS** (1930s Europe, 1940s US): REPLACEMENT ANIMATION, a different carved head or part per
  frame; about 9,000 carved pieces per short; the Smithsonian holds his "Yawning Man" with 27 faces
  (awn.com/heaven_and_hell/PAL/GP10.htm; americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_679379). Harryhausen's first job
  was at Pal's studio; Selick's Nightmare Before Christmas (1993) is "heavily inspired" by Pal.
- **JAN SVANKMAJER** (Czech, from 1964) and the **BROTHERS QUAY** (from 1979): found objects, meat, dolls, "a nightmare
  world" that discards narrative; "name-checked by just about every stop-motion animator coming up in the last 20
  years" (awn.com/animationworld/my-dinner-brothers-quay; skwigly.co.uk; chicagoreader.com). Selick: "I wanted to make
  independent films like Svankmajer's."
- **ADAM ELLIOT** ("clayography": Harvie Krumpet 2003, Oscar; Mary and Max 2009; Memoir of a Snail 2024): deliberately
  handmade, and he folds his own hereditary hand tremor into the work as unevenness (abc.net.au, 2024;
  pastemagazine.com/movies/adam-elliot/adam-elliot-clayography).
- **AARDMAN**: "the heart of our whole ethos is to keep everything handmade and keep the clay quality of it all, with
  the fingerprints and everything" (Nick Park, awn.com/animationworld/aardman-50-inside-clay-felt-and-fingerprints, 2026);
  Plasticine on metal armatures (theconversation.com, 2024).
- **HENRY SELICK on Wendell & Wild (2022)**: "I wanted to pull back from that sort of perfection and leave more
  mistakes in"; shot "not just on ones but also on twos, sometimes threes"; the replacement faces were LEFT WITH THEIR
  SEAM LINES visible (beforesandafters.com/2022/09/07/...; cartoonbrew.com/feature-film/henry-selick-wendell-and-wild-interview-222428.html;
  rogerebert.com, 2022: "sometimes it's 12, or 8" poses a second). This is the single best modern statement of the
  look Paolo fell for: polish taken OUT on purpose.

### THE THREE MOST LIKELY, IN ONE LINE EACH

1. CRASHBOX (HBO Family 1999 to 2000, Cuppa Coffee): the only mostly-stop-motion HBO kids show, hosted by a skeleton,
   Death and severed ears, in reruns for 25 years.
2. A LITTLE CURIOUS (HBO Family 1999 to 2000, Curious Pictures): clay and stop-motion household objects with faces, cut
   against photo and CG, aired beside Crashbox.
3. HBO FAMILY 411 (Cuppa Coffee claymation interstitials on the same channel): the texture between the shows.
Runner-up outside HBO: Pee-wee's Playhouse's Aardman "Penny" shorts, which memory often files under HBO.

Why the whole package is "creepy" in one sentence, from the literature: Mori's 1970 essay (Energy 7(4), 33 to 35;
IEEE Robotics and Automation Magazine 19(2), 2012, about 10,000 citations) says near-human things that are slightly
off feel eerie and that movement makes it worse; the 2016 work says the trigger is MIXED levels of realism on one
living thing; and stop-motion is physically all of those at once: a real object, lit by a real lamp, moving in steps,
with a swapped face. Inverse frames it as zombification, "seeing physical objects seem to come alive"
(inverse.com/article/19872-stop-motion-is-creepy-because-it-s-essentially-zombification). Collider adds that stilted
movement helps horror because the audience never has to separate the nightmare from reality: the artifice IS the
nightmare (collider.com/claymation-horror-movies/).

## PART 3: HOW THE 3D-TO-PIXEL RIG GETS THIS LOOK ON PURPOSE

The rig today (tools/bohemia_direction_master_rig.py, round three): Blender headless, Cycles, an orthographic camera,
eight facings (N NE E SE S SW W NW), one SUN from the upper left (tilt 40 degrees, turn minus 35, energy 3.0, angle 3
degrees), a dim sky fill, filter size 0.01 px, the model scaled to its true size at 98 painted px = 1.72 m (about 57 px
a metre), matte materials (roughness 0.9 or more, specular 0.15, metallic 0.2 or less), authored wear in metres (grime
from the model's own ambient occlusion, dust and sun-bleach on up-facing surfaces, a 20 cm colour drift, value grain
driven by the wear masks), clips sampled ONLY at the drawn keys (a walk is 2 beats = 6 keys, an idle 4 beats = 12 keys,
at 120 BPM; "nothing between two keys is rendered, because nothing between two keys is ever drawn"), a population snap
toward the colours the family's packs actually use, an 8-value ring, a contact shadow, and ONE crop box per model so
no frame jitters. That is already most of the recipe. What follows is each stop-motion ingredient as a setting the
rig can take, with a test a gate can read. Numbers marked PROPOSED are starting points for DIRECTION to tune on the
robot and the truck; numbers marked IN THE RIG are already there.

### 3.1 Step the animation to 12 a second with holds (IN THE RIG, extend it)
- The rig already renders only the drawn keys: 6 keys over 2 beats at 120 BPM is 6 keys a second; 12 keys over 4 beats
  is 3 a second. That is BELOW stop-motion's 12 a second, which is the right side to be on for this look (Selick:
  "sometimes 12, or 8"; below 12 "most people can detect jerkiness", en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_animation).
- Add HOLDS: a stop-motion animator holds a pose for 2 to 4 frames before a big move and after it lands. PROPOSED: a
  clip may declare hold keys (`--clip Walking:6:hold=1,4`) that repeat the previous key's render instead of sampling a
  new one. The envelope law stands (a joint may not reverse more than 120 degrees between drawn keys,
  gate an_envelope_ramps_slower_than_the_grid_gate.js), so a hold is a repeat, never a skipped sample.
- In Blender terms this is CONSTANT interpolation on the F-curves or the Stepped F-curve modifier
  (docs.blender.org/manual/en/latest/editors/graph_editor/fcurves/modifiers.html;
  docs.blender.org/manual/en/latest/editors/graph_editor/fcurves/properties.html); the rig does it by sampling frames
  directly, which is equivalent and simpler.
- TEST: every rendered frame of a clip equals one of the declared key frames; frame count = keys + holds; nothing else.

### 3.2 No motion blur, declared, not assumed (ADD ONE LINE)
- Cycles' motion blur is off by default, and the rig never turns it on, but it does not SET it off either. Blender
  itself documents that constant (stepped) keys plus sampled motion blur produce "a couple of frames of bullet time"
  blur because the step is read as high-speed motion (developer.blender.org/T57669; developer.blender.org/T73758).
  PROPOSED: `sc.render.use_motion_blur = False` written explicitly and recorded in rig.json. For EEVEE, if ever used:
  Max Blur 0 and Steps 1 (artisticrender.com/blender-eevee-motion-blur-guide/).
- TEST: rig.json carries motion_blur=false; the gate refuses a rig without the field.

### 3.3 Puppet jitter of 0 to 1 px, ON THE PUPPET, never on the camera (ADD, carefully)
- The rig's rule "facings never jitter by a sub-pixel; every picture of one model is cut with ONE box" is RIGHT and
  stays: a stop-motion CAMERA is bolted down, marked and never moved (stopmotionhero.com/stop-motion-lighting/). What
  wobbles is the PUPPET, because a hand touched it. The Blender Stop Motion Style Animator add-on exposes exactly this
  as a "jitter strength" beside its on-twos and on-threes step setting (extensions.blender.org/add-ons/stop-motion-style-animator/).
- PROPOSED: a seeded, per-key random offset of the model ROOT (and optionally each bone) of 0 to 1 cm in world space
  (at 57 px a metre that is 0 to 0.57 px, under one pixel, so it reads as a shimmer in the quantised edge, never as a
  slide), plus a rotation of 0 to 0.5 degrees. The ground pixel, the crop box and the contact shadow stay fixed. Seed
  by (model, clip, key, facing) so a render is repeatable and a gate can diff it.
- TEST: the ground pixel of every frame of a clip is identical; the body's bounding box differs from the previous key's
  by at most 1 px in each axis after the move itself is subtracted.

### 3.4 Clay shading: matte, with fingerprints the size of a real finger (IN THE RIG, add the print)
- IN THE RIG: roughness 0.9 or more, specular 0.15, metallic 0.2 or less ("the plastic highlight IS the toy").
- ADD the thumbprint: a fingerprint bump or normal map, tiled at a REAL thumb's size (a thumbprint is about 1.5 to 2 cm
  across; at 57 px a metre that is about 1 px, so it will read as grain, not as ridges, which is correct at sprite
  size and comes alive at portrait size, 128 px a metre, where it is 2 to 3 px). SouthernShotty's free clay shader
  tutorial ships a fingerprint texture and animates it so the prints move between frames (blendernation.com, 2019;
  classcentral.com/course/youtube-blender-clay-stop-motion-shader-tutorial-222983); Clay Doh does the same with cracks
  and dust and "surface imperfections shift and change over time" (superhivemarket.com/products/claydoh). The rig's own
  wear-mask rule applies: the print mask lives in METRES on the surface, strongest where a hand would grip (the head,
  the hands, the shoulders), weakest on flat panels; never an even noise layer (lesson 10: even grain is a leopard
  print; AH-01 R10: wear is authored, never a noise layer).
- OPTIONAL subsurface: a small subsurface weight (PROPOSED 0.05 to 0.1, radius about 1 cm) gives Plasticine's waxy
  depth; the paid shaders use Principled BSDF for this (superhivemarket.com/products/claydoh).
- TEST: the card's grain row stays inside 1.6 to 2.2 px a run at sprite size; at portrait size a print is visible on
  the head within 3 px of a grip point and absent on the chest panel.

### 3.5 Exaggerated head and hands (A MODEL RULE, not a render rule)
- Rankin/Bass put reused bodies under big heads; Pal's puppet is mostly head. PROPOSED model ratio for the game's
  people: head 1/5 to 1/4 of standing height (a real adult is about 1/7.5), hands 1.3 times real. The robot proxy
  already measures 0.751 m head on 1.72 m (0.44, far past this; it is a proof model, not the game's robot).
- TEST: the rig already measures the head mesh; rig.json's head_m / real height must land in 0.20 to 0.25 for a person,
  or the gate names it.

### 3.6 Replacement-face expressions, with the seam (IN THE RIG, add the seam)
- IN THE RIG: `--expression Angry` renders the portrait once per named shape key at FULL weight, no in-between. That is
  already a replacement face: the expression arrives all at once, which is the Puppetoon and Coraline method
  (americanhistory.si.edu; laikahiddenworlds.com/coraline).
- ADD: Selick left the seam lines in (beforesandafters.com, 2022). PROPOSED: a 1 px darker line at the replacement
  boundary (across the bridge of the nose and under the eyes, where Coraline's face plates split) drawn into the portrait
  pass; and on the sprite, the expression swap happens ON A BEAT, never between beats, so the face changes in one step
  like the body does.
- TEST: the portrait set for a head has N files for N expressions and no blends; the seam line is present at portrait
  size (128 px a metre) and absent at sprite size (it would be under a pixel).

### 3.7 One hard key light at a fixed angle, with its shadow (IN THE RIG)
- IN THE RIG: one sun, angle 3 degrees (a hard edge), upper left and above, plus a dim sky, and a contact shadow. That
  is the tabletop lamp. Keep the angle FIXED across every model forever: a stop-motion lamp is marked on the floor
  (stopmotionhero.com). PROPOSED: the sun's two angles and energy become constants in the style guide's twenty fields
  so the gate can refuse a render lit from anywhere else; the card's light row (top brighter than bottom) already
  tests the direction.
- A shallow depth of field is a MAP and FIGHT-BOARD choice, not a sprite choice: a sprite is one object in focus. The
  macro number (0.7 mm at 1:1 f/8) argues for a soft background behind the settlement screen's puppet rows, never for
  blur on a sprite. Not for the rig; noted for UI and COMBAT TWO.

### 3.8 The analog pass: chroma fringe and tracking, in the ENGINE, not baked into the sprite (A RULE)
- Analog horror is "low-fidelity graphics ... visual styles reminiscent of late 20th-century television and analog
  recordings" (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analog_horror); Local 58 (Kris Straub, 2015) is the usual origin
  (knowyourmeme.com/memes/cultures/analog-horror). The standard shader recipe: sample red and blue at opposite
  horizontal offsets for the fringe, a per-line jitter, a rolling tracking bar, grain
  (godotshaders.com/shader/vhs-crt-broadcast/; github.com/AlexMerzlikin/Unity-VHS-Shader; halisavakis.com/write-up-vhs-image-effect/).
  The one warning every guide gives: "heavy fringing will scream game filter instead of analog artifact"; keep it to a
  soft horizontal chroma blur with selective edge offset (pokie.website/diy-vhs-a-modder-s-guide...).
- WHY NOT IN THE SPRITE: a fringe baked into a 66 by 132 px sprite is a coloured outline, and the law says analog horror
  is thought about AT THE SOURCE, not a filter (laws/BOHEMIA_LAW_ANALOG_HORROR_FOR_EVERY_PIXEL_AND_EVERY_SOUND_9_20_26.md).
  The SOURCE-level analog is the matte clay, the hard lamp, the steps and the seams (3.1 to 3.7). The screen-level
  tape pass (PROPOSED: red/blue offset of 1 px at most, one tracking bar every 20 to 40 seconds, grain under 3 percent)
  belongs to the whole screen in the engine so the HUD, the map and the sprite wear the same tape. UI owns it.
- TEST: no sprite file carries a red or blue fringe on a grey edge (sample the ring: its hue must match the body's).

### 3.9 Palette and integer scale (IN THE RIG, keep it)
- IN THE RIG: the population snap pulls the render 0.6 of the way toward the colours the family's packs actually use
  (20,564 occupied bins), never toward a 64-colour list (lesson 6: his art is not limited-palette). Keep it: Dead Cells'
  pipeline, the known good 3D-to-pixel case, renders "at a very small size and without antialiasing" with a normal map
  and a basic toon shader, and leaves colour to the artist (gamedeveloper.com, Thomas Vasseur, 2018;
  gameanim.com/2018/01/31/dead-cells-3d-pipeline-2d-animation/). Lospec's palette tutorials are the reading aid for
  hue-shifting ramps (cool shadows, warm lights), not a snap target (lospec.com/pixel-art-tutorials/tags/palette).
- IN THE RIG: filter size 0.01 so one pixel is one pixel. Known Blender wrinkle: EEVEE at exactly 0.00 px went blurry in
  the viewport in 4.3 while the render stayed crisp (projects.blender.org/blender/blender/issues/130669); the rig's 0.01
  sidesteps it. Textures: interpolation Closest (nearest) (blenderartists.org/t/scale-pixel-art-video-without-blurring/1189951).
- INTEGER SCALING on screen: a 1.5x or 2.3x scale makes some pixel columns wider than others and the damage survives a
  scale back (divoom.com/blogs/setup-ideas/scale-pixel-art-without-blur; mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/5/2314, 2026). The rig
  renders at true size and "nothing is scaled after the render"; the ENGINE must draw sprites at whole multiples and the
  camera must land on whole pixels (THE GROUND MAY ZOOM, THE PERSON MAY NOT, 9/21). This is RUN's and PLUMBER's leg.
- TEST: the card's colour and top-eight rows (already gated); a screen capture at 1x shows every sprite pixel as an
  exact 1 by 1, 2 by 2 or 3 by 3 block.

### 3.10 One realism level per frame (A RULE from the 2016 papers)
- Realism inconsistency is the measured trigger (sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0010027715300755, 2016). The
  rig makes every character, beast, vehicle and prop from the same clay recipe, so they agree. The danger is the OLD
  flat sprites standing beside new clay ones during the changeover (rule 115 keeps the old sprites until each
  replacement passes VOTE). PROPOSED: the changeover goes BY SCENE, not by asset, where the demo allows it, so a fight
  board is all clay or all flat; and A Little Curious's trick (a photo texture beside clay) is used ONLY on purpose,
  for the phone's feed photos and the cracked iPhone, where the mixed realism is the point.
- TEST: EYES' five tells by hand per board until the art pipe gate reads the rig stamp on every sprite on a board.

## PART 4: ONE PAGE FOR THE ART DIRECTOR

### The ten-line recipe, applied to every character, beast, vehicle and prop

1. MODEL IT LIKE A PUPPET: real size in metres, head 1/5 to 1/4 of height, hands 1.3 times real, no seams the clay would
   not have (a truck is a carved block with panel lines pressed in, not a CAD body).
2. SURFACE IS MATTE MUD: roughness 0.9 or more, specular 0.15, metallic 0.2 or less. A highlight is a toy.
3. A HAND WAS HERE: fingerprint bump at a real thumb's size (1.5 to 2 cm), strongest at grip points, in metres on the
   surface, never an even noise.
4. DIRT IS AUTHORED: grime from the model's own crevices, dust on the up faces, a 20 cm colour drift. The 10/17 pass.
5. ONE LAMP, BOLTED DOWN: the sun at tilt 40, turn minus 35, angle 3 degrees, energy 3.0, a dim sky; the same on every
   model forever; a contact shadow under every standing thing.
6. MOVE IN STEPS: only the drawn keys (6 a walk, 12 an idle, at 120 BPM), holds declared, nothing between keys, motion
   blur written OFF in the file.
7. THE PUPPET WOBBLES, THE CAMERA DOES NOT: 0 to 1 cm seeded root jitter per key; the ground pixel, crop box and shadow
   never move.
8. THE FACE IS SWAPPED, NOT ANIMATED: one render per expression at full weight, the swap on a beat, the seam line kept at
   portrait size.
9. ONE PIXEL IS ONE PIXEL: filter 0.01, nearest textures, true size, the population snap to the family's own colours,
   whole-number scale on screen.
10. THE TAPE IS ON THE SCREEN, NOT THE SPRITE: fringe, tracking and grain in the engine over the whole frame, soft, so
    the HUD and the map and the puppet share one VHS.

### The five tells that a render has drifted out of the clay-and-tape world

1. A SHINE. Any specular hotspot on skin, cloth, fur or paint. The card's grain and top-eight rows catch it; the eye
   catches it first. Fix the material, not the paint.
2. A SMOOTH MOVE. An in-between frame, a blur trail, or a face that slides from one expression to another. The clip
   has more frames than keys plus holds, or motion blur crept on.
3. A LIGHT FROM ELSEWHERE. A shadow falling right or up, a lit underside, two shadows. The sun moved or a second light
   was added. The card's light row goes negative.
4. A CLEAN SURFACE. Under 2,330 colours a tile, an even flat panel with no crevice grime, or the opposite, a leopard
   print of even grain. Round two's exact failure (58 colours, 89 percent in the top eight). Dirt must sit in patches.
5. TWO WORLDS IN ONE FRAME. A flat old sprite beside a clay one, a photo texture on a thing that is not the phone, a
   fringe baked into one sprite's edge while its neighbours have none. The 2016 papers say this is the eeriness that
   turns on the player instead of the world; use it only where the phone is meant to feel like a foreign object.
