## THE CLOCK
- Raw meat in the heat is a race. Between 40 and 140 F the germs on it can double in as little as 20 minutes (S1). CORRECTED:
  20 minutes is the fastest they go, not a steady rate. The plain USDA rule: two hours out is the limit, ONE hour above 90 F (S1).
  A Las Vegas summer is over 90 F, so the one-hour rule is the one that counts.
- CORRECTED: the first draft said a fresh kill 'is rotten within a day'. No source gives a rot time. The one-hour rule is about
  germs that make you sick, which you cannot see or smell, not rot you can smell. So the question is still never 'can it be kept'
  but 'did somebody start in the first hour': every keeping method needs fresh meat, and salt cannot save meat that has already
  turned (S4). Hunters are told to skin the animal, get it into shade and cool it fast, and never wrap it in an airtight bag that
  holds the heat in (S46).
- GROUND meat spoils fastest of all (CONFIRMED): grinding mixes the germs from the outside all the way through (S2). Even in a
  fridge, ground meat keeps 1 to 2 days against 3 to 5 days for a steak (S3). A grinder without salt and smoke makes food that
  must be cooked right away and eaten; USDA cooks ground beef to 160 F (S2).

NOTES ON THE TABLE (what the sources add):
- DRYING, CORRECTED: US food-safety services say sun drying alone is NOT safe for meat. It never gets the meat hot enough to kill
  germs (S8, S9). USDA says to heat the strips to 160 F BEFORE drying (S5). Why: a dryer does not get the meat to 160 F before it
  dries, and germs that live through drying get much harder to kill with heat (S5). A short boil in the marinade or a 275 F oven
  for 10 minutes does it for quarter-inch strips (S7). Some extension services also accept heating the dried strips in an oven
  AFTER drying (S6), but USDA's main advice is before.
- DRYING, THE OLD WAY: the old open-air way (biltong) is real. Salted strips hang on a frame under insect netting (S12). It wants
  air under 80% humidity and a breeze, at about 77 to 86 F, for about a week to ten days (S13). The meat gets only 4 to 6 hours
  of direct sun, then finishes in shade, and its surface should stay under 104 F (S14). So the desert's dry air helps, but its
  summer sun is too hot to leave meat in all day. How much faster Las Vegas heat dries it is UNSOURCED.
- DRYING, WEIGHT: 'loses about two thirds of its weight' is CONFIRMED as a fair middle. One guide says 5 pounds dries to about 2
  (a 60% loss) (S10); leaner cuts dried harder lose up to three quarters.
- DRIED MEAT, PESTS: beetles get into dried meat 4 to 6 weeks after drying; smoking holds them off longer (S47).
- SALT, CORRECTED: plain salt slows botulism but its spores can live through a home salt cure (S19). Curing salt with nitrite in
  it is what stops botulism (S18). It is sold premixed; home cooks are told never to use pure nitrite (S19). The law caps nitrite
  at 1 ounce per 100 pounds of meat in a dry cure (S20). A dry rub is the way sized here; a salt-water brine (pickle) is the
  other way, with its own amounts (S20). Salt alone, with no sugar, is also fine (S15).
- SMOKE, CORRECTED: only meat already salted, cured or fermented should be cold smoked, because 85 F is inside the danger zone
  (S22). Smoke slows beetles on dried meat (S47) and keeps flies off while it dries (S23).
- PEMMICAN: it was packed in 41 kg (about 90 pound) hide bags and is a dense, high-energy food (S24). CORRECTED: the first draft
  called it 'the most food per pound a man can carry'; no source says 'the most'. Recipes vary from half and half to more fat.
- SAUSAGE: botulism was first named 'sausage poisoning' (S27). A dry salami outbreak in Canada made at least 143 people sick
  (S27). E. coli and Listeria can live through home fermenting and drying (S28). CONFIRMED: done wrong, it kills.
- CANNING: meat is a low-acid food and must be pressure canned (S31). A boiling water pot only reaches 212 F, which is not hot
  enough (S32). Botulism in a bad jar cannot be seen, smelled or tasted (S48). A weighted-gauge canner runs at 10 pounds of
  pressure up to 1,000 feet, 15 pounds above that (S29). Electric pressure pots are not recommended (S33). A hot storeroom over
  95 F breaks the safe-storage rule (S34).
- COLD, THE MATH: a typical ENERGY STAR chest freezer uses about 215 kWh a year (S37), which is 0.59 kWh a day. A big 21.7 cubic
  foot one uses 346 kWh a year (S38), which is 0.95 kWh a day. One rechargeable AA (2000 mAh at 1.2 V) holds 2.4 Wh (S39).
  0.59 kWh is 590 Wh, divided by 2.4 Wh = about 245 AA charges a day. 0.95 kWh = about 395 a day. A full 1 kWh would be about
  417. Charger losses would make it worse (not counted; UNSOURCED). These ratings are lab numbers, so a freezer in a hot garage
  likely uses more (UNSOURCED, no measured figure found).

## WHAT DECIDES IT IS SALT AND THE FIRST HOUR, AND A SHORT FIRE IS THE SAFETY STEP (CORRECTED)
- CORRECTED: the first draft said fire only helps. By the USDA rule, fire (or a pot of boiling water) is the step that kills the
  germs before drying (S5). The sun does the drying; the fire does the killing. Smoke also dries, coats and keeps flies off (S23).
- What a household runs out of is still SALT and the first hour. Salting before drying holds back both germs and insects (S12).
- WHERE THE SALT IS IN THIS VALLEY: pool stores and garages hold 40-pound bags of pool salt and water-softener salt (S42; that
  every pool store and many garages have them is UNSOURCED). It IS almost pure salt: over 99% (S41, S44). CORRECTED: but the
  makers label it not food grade and not for eating (S40, S42). Softener pellets are not food grade even when pressed from
  food-grade salt (S43), and some softener salts carry an added rust fighter (S44). No source calls it safe to eat; a desperate
  household would use it at its own risk. At the extension rate, one 40-pound bag cures about 500 pounds of meat (CORRECTED from
  '400 to 800'). Past that, the dry lake beds: Nevada's dry lakes do hold salt (S45), but none was found named in the Las Vegas
  valley, and whether that salt is safe to eat is UNSOURCED.
- So yes: everyday people can keep meat, cheaply and fast, with a knife, a screen, a pot of boiling water, the sun and salt.
  Cold storage is the rich man's way and it burns money every day.
