## THE HEADLINE, AND IT IS THE PRAISE AND THE CRITICISM POINTING AT THE SAME FACT

**The most praised open world in games has some of the least free missions in it.**

The world is deeply reactive. The missions are a corridor. Both halves are true, and
together they tell you exactly where the greatness lives: **in the ambient systems, not
in the scripted content.** People remember the horse, the camp, the weather and the
stranger on the road. Nobody remembers mission 43.

---

## 1. THE CORES SYSTEM — the most distinctive mechanic, and the least copied

Three resources — Health, Stamina, Dead Eye — and **each one has two layers**:

- **The BAR** (the ring around the icon) is the moment-to-moment meter. Sprinting
  drains the stamina bar. Getting shot drains the health bar.
- **The CORE** (the icon in the middle) is your long-term condition. **It drains
  passively — about 90 real minutes from full to empty even if you do nothing**, faster
  if you are cold or badly dressed.

**And here is the part that makes it good: your CORE level sets how fast your BAR
refills.** Full cores mean bars snap back. Low cores mean recovery crawls. If a bar
empties completely, further drain starts eating the core underneath it.

Why that is clever: it separates **"am I winded right now"** from **"how am I doing
today"** — and it makes the long-term state govern the short-term one instead of being
another gauge to stare at. That is why eating, sleeping and dressing for weather matter
without a nagging hunger meter. You never watch the core. You notice your stamina
stopped coming back.

**Dead Eye breaks the pattern on purpose:** its bar does *not* regenerate from its core
at all. You refill it with tonics, or by a chance roll on a kill. So one of the three
is a consumable and the other two are conditions — three resources, three different
relationships to time.

## 2. HONOR IS VISIBLE, AND IT HAS AN EXCHANGE RATE

Honor is a meter you can see, and it is priced in real money: **rank 2 gives 10% off in
all stores, rank 5 gives 25%, and +7 gives 50% off.** It also changes dialogue, some
mission variants, and the ending.

The notable inversion: in most games being bad is more profitable. Here **being good is
literally cheaper** — a high-honor player buys the expensive horse for half price. The
morality system pays out in the economy rather than only in cutscenes.

## 3. THE CAMP IS A SOCIAL FEEDBACK LOOP, NOT A BASE

Two interlinked systems: a **donation box** for communal funds, and a **ledger** for
purchased upgrades (wagons, lodging, tools).

Donating money and items raises funds **and morale** — and morale is a separate system
that changes:
- what companions say to you
- **which companions show up for chores**
- which side conversations happen on breaks
- some mission availability
- **whether gang members themselves donate to the box**

That last one is a real loop: you put in, morale rises, they put in.

And a nice friction detail: **each wagon upgrade makes restocking it cost more.** The
upgrade is not free forever; it raises your running cost.

What makes it work is that **the output is dialogue, not a number.** You do not watch a
morale bar. You notice people are talking to each other.

## 4. WITNESSES — the crime system, and its best idea is the delay

- Most crimes generate **nothing at all without a witness.**
- **Two separate bits**: a white eye means someone saw it; a red eye means they know it
  was **you**. A bandana breaks identification without breaking witnessing.
- The witness has to **physically travel** to reach a lawman. **The consequence is in
  transit** — and that window is where the gameplay is. You can chase them, threaten
  them and they forget, or let them go and take the bounty.

## 5. THE DETAIL LAYER — why it reads as "greatest of all time"

None of it is a mechanic; all of it is consistency. Mud accumulates and washes off.
Beard grows. Weapons degrade and need cleaning. Food spoils. Arthur gains and loses
weight from what you eat. Horses bond with you over time and behave differently at
higher bond levels.

The lesson is not the list. It is that **the world keeps its promises at a scale
nobody asked it to.** Once a player catches the game being consistent about something
trivial, they start trusting it about everything.

## AND NOW THE CRITICISM, WHICH IS THE MORE USEFUL HALF

**The story missions are famously, painfully linear**, and this is the single most
common complaint from people who otherwise love it:

- **Deviating from the scripted path fails you.** Move to the wrong cover and it is a
  fail state.
- The most-cited example: in the first train robbery, **killing someone fails the
  mission** even though threatening passengers is permitted.
- Critics describe the mission design as if open-world mission design "hadn't evolved
  since 2008," and note that the open world is only really embraced in the *side*
  activities — hunting, fishing, wandering.

So the game contains a contradiction: an enormously reactive world you are free to
exist in, wrapped around missions that punish you for improvising. **The freedom and
the story never actually meet.**

## THE THING WORTH UNDERSTANDING FROM ALL OF IT

**Its reputation is built almost entirely on the parts with no author in the room.**
The cores, the camp morale, the witnesses, the mud, the horse — systems that respond to
whatever you happen to do. The parts that were hand-authored scene by scene are the
parts people complain about.

That is the actual lesson of RDR2, and it is a lesson about where to spend effort, not
a feature to copy.
