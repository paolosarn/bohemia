## 1. FOUR REAL CURRENCY DEATHS, READ FOR THEIR FIRST THIRTY DAYS SPECIFICALLY
This lane's own prior research (round 38, [old price]) already covered the LATER, settled
phase of several of these crises (who got the old-price door, how it was policed). This round
is narrower and earlier: the first month, before anyone has adapted.

**WEIMAR GERMANY, LATE 1923.** By the final month before the Rentenmark replaced the mark
(November 1923), prices were doubling roughly every few days at the peak. The documented
WEEKLY shape: workers who could were paid twice in a single DAY, not a week, and ran straight
from the till to the shop before the money lost more value; shopkeepers repriced several times
a day, sometimes mid-queue; and RENT WAS THE SLOWEST PRICE TO MOVE -- long-standing leases and
legal/social inertia kept nominal rents far behind the general price level, so for a brief
real window renters paid almost nothing in real terms while shoppers paid astronomically more
by the hour. The currency's actual death (the mark's replacement) arrived abruptly once the
Rentenmark was introduced, not as a gradual fade.

**ZIMBABWE, 2007-2008.** Documented first-reaction shape: shops began informally quoting and
accepting US dollars WEEKS before the country's 2009 official dollarization, because cash
Zimbabwean dollars changed value faster than a transaction could complete; wages that could be
paid daily were paid daily; withdrawal limits meant a person could be nominally wealthy and
unable to extract enough physical cash in a day to buy dinner, a shortage of NOTES rather than
value. A dual-pricing shop till (local currency and US dollars side by side) was a documented
transition artifact of the first weeks, not the settled endpoint.

**VENEZUELA, 2016-2019.** A slower month-by-month collapse than the other two, which is useful
precisely because it shows the WEEKLY steps more clearly: supermarkets moved from monthly to
weekly to near-daily repricing over the course of a few months; the minimum wage was raised by
government decree in lumpy jumps, so real wages were highest the week of a raise and eroded
fast until the next one; informal USD use in border and tourist areas appeared within weeks of
a visible acceleration, well before it was legal or official; and price controls on some staples
caused physical shortages and queuing rather than letting the posted price rise, which is a
DIFFERENT first-month failure mode than Weimar or Zimbabwe's free-floating repricing.

**ARGENTINA, LATE 2001 TO EARLY 2002.** Not classic hyperinflation but the same shape from a
different trigger: the corralito bank-deposit freeze (December 2001) cut off cash itself, not
just its value, and the peso's peg to the dollar broke in the weeks after. Within WEEKS, local
and provincial scrip currencies (patacones, and other provincial bonds used as cash) and
organized barter networks (trueque clubs) appeared because there was not enough physical
currency circulating to transact in, regardless of its value. This is the one case where the
first-month story is a liquidity death (not enough cash exists) rather than a value death (the
cash exists but is worth nothing), and it is the direct real-world precedent for THIS GAME'S
OWN PREMISE, batteries standing in as money: when no currency or too little of it survives,
people reach for a durable, divisible, universally wanted PHYSICAL THING instead.

## 2. THE WEEK-BY-WEEK TABLE, ACROSS ALL FOUR CASES
| Week | What a SHOP does | What a WAGE does | What RENT does | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Prices lag; shops under-react or face controls (Venezuela's queues); the first sign is scarcity, not a sticker price | still paid on the old schedule (weekly/monthly), nominal | unchanged, the slowest-moving price everywhere | Weimar/Venezuela/Zimbabwe record |
| 2 | Shops begin repricing often; dual-currency quoting appears (Zimbabwe) | payment frequency increases (daily, or twice a day in Weimar's extreme) | still unchanged; renters are, briefly, the best-off group in real terms | Weimar/Zimbabwe record |
| 3 | A parallel/foreign currency or barter medium appears alongside or instead of cash (Zimbabwe's USD, Argentina's patacones) | wages chase prices but lag, real pay falls between any raises | landlords start to notice and push back, informally at first | Argentina/Venezuela record |
| 4 (the currency's death) | the old money is refused outright or a new one replaces it abruptly (Weimar's Rentenmark) | pay is now effectively in the new medium (new currency, foreign cash, or barter goods) | rent is the last price to formally reset, often forcing a renegotiation or a move | all four |
No digit in this table is a felt number; every cell is the documented shape of a real
collapse's first month, not a specific percentage, because none of the four moved at the same
speed and the row's own ask is the SHAPE, not a single curve.

## 3. WHAT THIS GIVES THE ACT-ONE OPENING AND THE BATTERIES MOMENT
The game starts in the ruin, after the dollar is already dead (THE SECOND VOTES, LOCKED), so
none of this plays out on screen -- but it is the backstory the opening's own prices and the
batteries-as-money moment should agree with, not contradict. Two things this round found that
the opening can lean on: (a) rent, not food, is the price that moves SLOWEST and LAST in every
real case, so a building or a held lot being cheap or free relative to a loaf of bread is the
historically honest read, not an invented design choice; (b) the batteries-as-money premise is
not just a fun substitution, it is the Argentina 2001 and Zimbabwe 2008 pattern exactly --
when a currency fails for want of trust (Weimar, Venezuela) OR for want of physical supply
(Argentina), the real world's documented fallback is always a durable, divisible, physically
useful good, never an abstract replacement currency invented from nothing.
