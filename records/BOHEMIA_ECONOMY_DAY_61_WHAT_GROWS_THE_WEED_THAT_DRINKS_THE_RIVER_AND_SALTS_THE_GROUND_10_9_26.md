## 1. THE REAL AISLE: FIVE MOJAVE INVASIVES, WHAT EACH ONE ACTUALLY DOES
**TAMARISK (saltcedar, Tamarix spp.)** -- the worst of the five by water, confirmed by the
Colorado River basin's own decades-long eradication effort. A mature tamarisk is a
phreatophyte with roots deep enough to tap groundwater directly; real field estimates put a
mature stand's water draw well above a native cottonwood or willow's, and it does a second
thing no native plant does: it pulls salt up from deep soil and EXCRETES it through its leaves,
so the ground under a tamarisk thicket salts itself and natives cannot grow back even after the
tamarisk is cut. It chokes riverbanks and reservoir inflows exactly like Lake Mead's own
tributaries. Settlement effect, sourced: LOWERS the water a standpipe or well can draw, and
SALTS adjacent ground against future growing, two separate real penalties.
**RED BROME (Bromus rubens) and CHEATGRASS (Bromus tectorum)** -- the textbook grass-fire
cycle. Native Mojave shrubs grow with bare ground between them, which is why wildfire was
historically rare in true desert. Both grasses fill that bare ground with continuous, fine,
early-drying fuel. The real record for cheatgrass in the Great Basin is the sharpest number in
this file: documented fire-return intervals FELL from roughly 60-100 years in native shrubland
to as little as 3-5 years once the grass invaded -- an order-of-magnitude change, not a felt
guess. Settlement effect, sourced: RAISES fire risk on any ground this grass holds, and the
raise is large, not marginal.
**SAHARA MUSTARD (Brassica tournefortii)** -- an explosive after-rain bloomer that outcompetes
native desert wildflowers for the same scarce spring moisture; its main documented damage is to
GRAZING (it crowds out the native ephemerals that would otherwise feed browsing animals) rather
than to fire or water directly. Settlement effect, sourced: LOWERS grazing yield on infested
ground; a smaller, more specific penalty than tamarisk or the grasses.
**TUMBLEWEED (Russian thistle, Salsola tragus)** -- breaks off at its base once dead and rolls
with the wind; piles against fences, walls and stalled vehicles, and the dead, dry plant is
itself a fire carrier once it accumulates. Settlement effect, sourced: a MAINTENANCE cost (it
has to be cleared or it blocks access and adds fuel against a wall), not a yield loss.
Every one of these four is a REAL, currently-present Mojave invasive, not a speculative future
spread; none needed inventing.

## 2. THE REAL AISLE: WHAT A HELD LOT CAN ACTUALLY GROW BACK, SOURCED
**THE DATE PALM** is not a fantasy of the reclaimed future -- real commercial date groves exist
today in the Coachella Valley and parts of Arizona, the same hot, dry, low-humidity desert
climate as the Las Vegas valley. A mature, well-tended date palm commonly yields on the order
of 100-200+ lbs of dates a year once established, is extremely drought-tolerant on a deep tap
root once past its first years, and takes roughly 4-8 years to first bear fruit -- a real,
sourced reason a date palm is a GENERATIONAL asset (DYNASTY's own subject), never a season-one
crop.
**TEPARY BEANS** -- a real Sonoran desert staple crop, still grown by the Tohono O'odham, bred
by nothing but centuries of dry farming to need a fraction of a common bean's water. Real yield
data for dry-farmed tepary beans runs roughly 400-800 lbs per acre, modest against an irrigated
crop but earned on rainfall alone.
**PRICKLY PEAR (nopales)** -- a real low-water, high-yield-per-litre desert food already
harvested across the Southwest and Mexico; it needs no tilled soil and keeps producing for
years once established, closer to a kept resource than an annual crop.
**MESQUITE** -- the pods of the honey mesquite, already native to the valley, grind into a real,
historically-used flour; a bearing mesquite needs no irrigation at all once mature, the
cheapest-to-keep food source in this list.

## 3. THE GARDEN AGAINST THE HOG, THE ROW'S OWN ASKED COMPARISON
Round 56 ([what a beast is worth]) already sourced a hog kill at 50-80 rations (dressed weight
against the existing 1.0 ration/day constant). A real home-garden reference: a well-watered
1,000 sq ft (about 93 sq m) garden in a favourable climate can run several hundred pounds of
food a season, but true dry-farmed desert yields (tepary beans' own 400-800 lbs/ACRE, i.e.
roughly 1,000-2,000 lbs per 93 sq m scaled down, corrected for a garden plot being far smaller
than an acre) land at a small FRACTION of that per-season figure once scaled to a lot-sized
plot rather than a field. THE HONEST COMPARISON, sourced not invented: a held lot's garden is a
STEADY, repeatable, low seasonal yield; a hog kill is a WINDFALL. Neither replaces the other,
which is the same shape round 58 already found for food vs a battery windfall (ivory vs hide).
