# BOHEMIA COORDINATOR SCHOOL, ROUND 10 OF 10: TALKING TO THE BOSS (10/10/26)

## THE QUESTION (one paragraph)
How do the best communicators report up to a busy, non-technical decision maker who reads on a phone? Paolo reads from the bottom of the screen up, at eighth-grade level, and has said the same three things for months: replies too long, status that reads like a log, and "I didn't see nothing new" after a ship. This round studied the traditions built for exactly that reader: military and intelligence BLUF, Amazon's narrative memo and PowerPoint ban, Red/Amber/Green status and its watermelon failure, plain-language research in the US and UK, how Pixar, Nintendo and Valve show progress instead of describing it, Nielsen Norman Group's reading research, and how to deliver bad news without burying it. Machine note: five searches ran before the budget closed and the proxy refused direct fetches. (confirmed) means this round's searches; (standing) means a primary source I could not re-open, so it is paraphrased, never quoted.

## WHAT I FOUND

1. BLUF is a regulation, not a style tip. US Army Regulation 25-50 requires two things of all Army writing: the main point first ("bottom line up front") and the active voice. The Air Force's Tongue and Quill carries the same rule. (confirmed) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BLUF_(communication) and https://matthewstrom.com/writing/bluf. Why it matters here: the Army made "decision before story" enforceable. Our replies still open with what we did.

2. Amazon banned slides by email on June 9, 2004. Bezos's 2017 shareholder letter (April 2018) says senior meetings open with everyone silently reading a six-page narrative memo; talk starts only when reading ends. His reason: slides leave out information and are not logically connected. (confirmed) https://www.anecdote.com/2018/05/amazons-six-page-narrative-structure/ and https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2017-letter-to-shareholders. Why it matters here: a bullet list hides a missing argument. Write the one paragraph that proves the lanes' work hangs together; never forward their bullets.

3. Status reports lie upward, and they lie optimistic. A 2014 MIT Sloan Management Review article ("The Pitfalls of Project Status Reporting," Keil, Smith, Iacovou, Thompson) reviewed fourteen studies; in one, 56 software project managers' reports were biased 60 percent of the time, more than twice as often optimistic as pessimistic. (secondary confirmation: https://itnext.io/software-development-has-a-watermelon-problem-f47e023ee443; primary: https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/the-pitfalls-of-project-status-reporting/) Why it matters here: 26 lanes each want to look green. Passing on lane self-reports passes on a 60 percent error rate.

4. Watermelon reporting is the named failure of Red/Amber/Green: green outside, red all the way through. The cures every source agrees on: a numeric threshold per colour ("25 percent over budget equals red"), a safe way to say red, and a fixed rhythm. (confirmed) https://bestoutcome.com/knowledge-centre/is-your-project-a-watermelon-project-or-a-kiwi-fruit and https://www.fluid.work/blog/rag-status-impact-project-reporting. Why it matters here: "all gates green" is our watermelon. A colour with no number behind it is an opinion.

5. GOV.UK writes for a reading age of 9, as a benchmark, not an insult. Children know the 5,000 most common words by about 9 and then read them by shape; adults still read those words fastest. The literacy figure behind it: 1 in 7 adults in England read at or below the level of a 9 to 11 year old. (confirmed) https://www.gov.uk/guidance/content-design/writing-for-gov-uk and https://stormid.com/blog/content-accessibility-part-1-speak-plainly/. The GOV.UK sentence cap is 25 words (standing). Why it matters here: Paolo's eighth-grade rule is generous, and most of our sentences to him run past 25 words.

6. US plain language is law and it is measurable. The Plain Writing Act of 2010 binds federal agencies; plainlanguage.gov's guidelines open with "write for your audience" and "put the main message first," and recommend sentences averaging about 20 words with one idea each. Flesch-Kincaid grade is 0.39 times words per sentence plus 11.8 times syllables per word minus 15.59; the Hemingway editor flags hard sentences and recommends grade 9 or lower. (standing) https://www.plainlanguage.gov/guidelines/ and https://hemingwayapp.com/. Why it matters here: our reply gate can compute this number on the words that land on his screen and refuse anything over grade 8.

7. People read almost nothing on a screen. Nielsen Norman Group's 2008 analysis of 25 users across 59,573 page views: on an average visit users read at most 28 percent of the words, more likely 20 percent, and spend about 4.4 extra seconds per extra 100 words. (standing) https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/. Why it matters here: a 600-word reply gets about 120 words read. Decide which 120.

8. The F-pattern: eyes read the first lines fully, then scan the first two or three words of each line down the left edge. Found in 2006, re-tested in 2017, still holds on mobile. (standing) https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/. Why it matters here: with a bottom-up reader the F is upside down. The first two words of each of the last five lines are the reply.

9. The phone is not the excuse; length is. NN/g's 2016 study found comprehension on phones about equal to big screens (slightly higher, not significant) and reading slightly slower; the 19 versus 39 percent gap that blogs quote is an older University of Alberta study, not NN/g. (confirmed) https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content/ via https://uxmag.com/articles/why-reading-on-mobile-is-uniquely-challenging. Why it matters here: Paolo can understand anything we write. He will not read anything long.

10. Pixar shows unfinished work every day. Catmull's 2008 Harvard Business Review article describes dailies (incomplete shots shown to the director and crew) and the Braintrust (rough cuts of the whole film); showing early kills the fear of judgment and gets the director's correction while it is cheap. (standing) https://hbr.org/2008/09/how-pixar-fosters-collective-creativity. Why it matters here: this is COOK EVERY ROUND (9/21) in another studio's words. The director sees the thing, not a sentence about the thing.

11. Nintendo and Valve judge by watching play. The Iwata Asks interviews describe Miyamoto handing a controller to someone who has never seen the game and watching in silence; his "upending the tea table" came from playing a build, not reading a report. Valve's Half-Life cabal (Ken Birdwell, Game Developer, 1999) put a fresh player on the build every week while designers watched without talking. (standing) https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/ and https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-cabal-valve-s-design-process-for-creating-i-half-life-i-. Why it matters here: "I didn't see nothing new" is a tea table flipping over a report. The build must change where his thumb lands, and the report is one line pointing at one tab.

12. Bad news has a medical format. SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) came from US Navy submarines and was adopted by Kaiser Permanente for nurses reporting to doctors: worst fact first, recommendation attached, under a minute. (standing) https://www.ihi.org/resources/tools/sbar-tool-situation-background-assessment-recommendation. Why it matters here: when a deploy breaks, the red belongs in a fixed block at the bottom, not in the middle of a status paragraph.

## WHAT THE BEST DO THAT WE DON'T

- They put the decision where the eye lands. For Paolo that is the bottom. Our ask block is there by law, but the line above it is often the proof line, so his first read is jargon.
- They let one number carry the status: 25 words, 6 pages, 28 percent. We say "a lot shipped" and "mostly green." He cannot picture that.
- They never report green without a threshold. We report lane rows done because a gate passed; the research says 60 percent of those are rosy.
- They show the build and shut up. We write a paragraph about the thing and name the tab at the end. The tab name should be the first words of the line.
- They cut, they do not simplify tone. About 20 percent gets read; our replies land at 500 to 900 words, so the 700 words above the TLDR are why he scrolls past the ask.
- They deliver bad news in a fixed shape, worst fact first. We have no shape for it.

## THE CHANGES FOR THE COORDINATOR

1. Hard cap, measured. Every reply to Paolo is at most 150 words from first line to play link; the reply gate computes Flesch-Kincaid on those words and refuses anything over grade 8.0 or 150 words. Test: the gate prints both numbers; a failing reply does not send.
2. The last five lines carry the whole message, in order: the changed thing with its TAB name as the first two words; the number that proves it; the one red or "No red"; WHAT I NEED FROM YOU; the two-sentence bottom line. Test: cover everything above them; if he would lose a decision, the reply is wrong.
3. No colour without a number. "Done," "green" and "shipped" may only appear in a sentence that also holds a measured digit, and "red" means a miss of a threshold written down before the round started. Test: grep the reply for those words; each sentence must contain a digit.
4. Bad news in SBAR shape, always directly above the ask: worst fact, cause, what I did, what I recommend, one sentence each. Test: a failure mentioned anywhere else and not in this block fails the gate.
5. The reply template, 12 lines maximum, every reply:

   ROUND DONE. (lanes that shipped, lanes that did not, two numbers)
   TAB NAME: the one new thing to look at, one sentence, one number.
   TAB NAME: a second new thing, or delete this line.
   RED: the worst fact in one sentence, or "No red this round."
   CAUSE: one sentence. FIX: one sentence. (delete if no red)
   proof: paths and gate names, one line, machine-only
   **WHAT I NEED FROM YOU**
   1. (one word answers it) or "Nothing, I'm good."
   Bottom line: what I just did, one sentence.
   What you do with it and why it matters, one sentence.

## ONE RULE
THE LAST FIVE LINES ARE THE REPLY: write the change, the one number, the one red, the ask and the two-sentence bottom line as the last five lines he sees, cap the whole reply at 150 words and grade 8, and treat every word above those lines as a word he will not read.

## FOR PAOLO, IN PLAIN WORDS
The best bosses in the world get the answer first, in short words, with one number, and they get shown the thing instead of told about it. From now on every reply to you is under 150 words and the last five lines carry everything: what changed, which tab, what broke, what I need, and the bottom line. If you ever have to scroll up to find a decision, I did it wrong.
