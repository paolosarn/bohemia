# BOHEMIA COORDINATOR SCHOOL, ROUND 10 OF 10: TALKING TO THE BOSS (10/10/26)

## THE QUESTION (one paragraph)
How do the best communicators report up to a busy, non-technical decision maker who reads on a phone? Paolo reads from the bottom of the screen up, at eighth-grade level, and has said the same three things for months: the replies are too long, they read like a log, and after a ship he "didn't see nothing new." This round studied the traditions built for exactly that reader: military and intelligence BLUF, Amazon's narrative memo and its PowerPoint ban, Red/Amber/Green status and its watermelon failure, plain-language research (US and UK), how Pixar and Nintendo show progress instead of describing it, mobile reading research from Nielsen Norman Group, how game studios report through builds, and how to deliver bad news without burying it. Network note for the machine: five web searches ran before the search budget closed and the proxy refused every direct page fetch; findings marked (confirmed) came from this round's searches, findings marked (standing knowledge) are well-known primary sources I could not re-open this round, and no quotes are given for those.

## WHAT I FOUND

1. BLUF is a regulation, not a style tip. US Army Regulation 25-50 (Preparing and Managing Correspondence) requires two things of every piece of Army writing: the main point goes first ("bottom line up front") and the active voice. The Air Force handbook The Tongue and Quill carries the same rule. (confirmed) Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BLUF_(communication) and https://matthewstrom.com/writing/bluf. Why it matters here: the Army decided that a reader with no time gets the decision before the story, and made it enforceable. Our replies still often open with what we did and end with what he needs.

2. Amazon banned slides by email on June 9, 2004 and replaced them with "narratively structured" memos. Bezos's 2017 shareholder letter (published April 2018) says senior meetings start with everyone silently reading a six-page memo, and discussion begins only when the reading is done. His reason: slides leave out information and are not logically connected. (confirmed) Sources: https://www.anecdote.com/2018/05/amazons-six-page-narrative-structure/ and https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2017-letter-to-shareholders. Why it matters here: Amazon's point is that the writing forces the thinking; a bullet list hides a missing argument. Our lanes send bullet logs. The coordinator should write the one paragraph that proves the lanes' work hangs together, and not forward the bullets.

3. Status reports lie upward, and they lie optimistic. A 2014 MIT Sloan Management Review article (Keil, Smith, Iacovou and Thompson, "The Pitfalls of Project Status Reporting") reviewed fourteen studies; in one, 56 software project managers' status reports were biased 60 percent of the time, and the bias was more than twice as likely to be optimistic as pessimistic. (secondary confirmation via https://itnext.io/software-development-has-a-watermelon-problem-f47e023ee443; primary: https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/the-pitfalls-of-project-status-reporting/) Why it matters here: 26 lanes each want to look green. A coordinator who passes on lane self-reports is passing on a 60 percent error rate.

4. Watermelon reporting is the named failure of Red/Amber/Green: green on the outside, red all the way through. The known cures are objective thresholds for each colour (for example "25 percent over budget equals red"), a safe way to report bad news, and a fixed reporting rhythm. (confirmed) Sources: https://bestoutcome.com/knowledge-centre/is-your-project-a-watermelon-project-or-a-kiwi-fruit and https://www.fluid.work/blog/rag-status-impact-project-reporting. Why it matters here: "all gates green" is our watermelon. The 7/26 STOP PRODUCING law already says green gates are never an argument; this round says why: a colour with no numeric threshold behind it is an opinion.

5. GOV.UK writes for a reading age of 9, and it is a benchmark, not an insult to the reader. The guidance says children learn the 5,000 most common words by about age 9 and then stop reading them letter by letter and recognise their shape; adults still read those words fastest. The National Literacy Trust figure behind it: 1 in 7 adults in England read at or below the level expected of a 9 to 11 year old. (confirmed) Sources: https://www.gov.uk/guidance/content-design/writing-for-gov-uk and https://stormid.com/blog/content-accessibility-part-1-speak-plainly/. Why it matters here: Paolo's eighth-grade rule is actually generous. The GOV.UK sentence cap is 25 words (standing knowledge). Most of our sentences to him run past that.

6. US plain language is federal law. The Plain Writing Act of 2010 requires agencies to write so the public can find, understand and use what it reads; the plainlanguage.gov guidelines open with "write for your audience" and "put the main message first," and recommend short sentences averaging around 20 words with one idea each. (standing knowledge) Source: https://www.plainlanguage.gov/guidelines/. Why it matters here: "one idea per sentence" is the single edit that would fix most of our replies.

7. The readability number can be measured, every reply, by a machine. Flesch-Kincaid grade level is 0.39 times average words per sentence plus 11.8 times average syllables per word minus 15.59; the Hemingway editor flags sentences as hard or very hard and recommends aiming for grade 9 or lower. (standing knowledge) Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flesch%E2%80%93Kincaid_readability_tests and https://hemingwayapp.com/. Why it matters here: our reply gate already exists; it should compute this number on the words that land on his screen and refuse anything over grade 8.

8. People read almost nothing on a screen. Nielsen Norman Group's 2008 analysis of 25 users across 59,573 page views found that on an average visit users read at most 28 percent of the words, more likely 20 percent, and spend only about 4.4 extra seconds for every extra 100 words. (standing knowledge) Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/. Why it matters here: a 600-word reply gets about 120 words read. Decide which 120.

9. The F-pattern: eyes read the first lines fully, then scan down the left edge for the first two or three words of each line. The 2006 eyetracking finding was re-tested in 2017 and still holds, including on mobile. (standing knowledge) Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/. Why it matters here: with Paolo reading bottom-up, the F is upside down. The first two words of each of the last five lines are the whole reply.

10. Phones do not lower comprehension any more, but they do slow reading. NN/g's 2016 study found comprehension on phones was about the same as on big screens (slightly higher, not significant) and reading was slightly slower; the older 19 percent versus 39 percent comprehension gap that marketing blogs quote comes from a University of Alberta study, not NN/g. (confirmed) Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content/ via https://uxmag.com/articles/why-reading-on-mobile-is-uniquely-challenging. Why it matters here: the phone is not the excuse. Length is. Paolo can understand anything we write; he will not read anything long.

11. Pixar's dailies: every animator shows unfinished work to the director and the whole crew every day, and the Braintrust reviews a rough cut of the whole film every few months. Catmull's 2008 Harvard Business Review article argues that showing incomplete work kills the fear of being judged and gets the director's correction early, while it is cheap. (standing knowledge) Source: https://hbr.org/2008/09/how-pixar-fosters-collective-creativity. Why it matters here: this is COOK EVERY ROUND (9/21) in another studio's words. The director sees the thing, not a sentence about the thing.

12. Nintendo's directors judge by playing. In the Iwata Asks interviews, Miyamoto's habit of handing a controller to someone who has never seen the game and watching them in silence is described repeatedly, and his "upending the tea table" (chabudai gaeshi) happened when he played a build, not when he read a report. (standing knowledge) Source: https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/. Why it matters here: Paolo's "I didn't see nothing new" is a tea table flipping over a report. The build must change where his thumb lands.

13. Valve's Half-Life "cabal" process (Ken Birdwell, Game Developer, 1999) put a fresh player in front of the build every week and had the designers watch without talking; written design docs were secondary to what the playtest showed. (standing knowledge) Source: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-cabal-valve-s-design-process-for-creating-i-half-life-i-. Why it matters here: "weekly playable, watched, not described" is the industry's answer to status. Our weekly unit is the round and the demo link; the report should be one line pointing at one tab.

14. Bad news has a medical format. SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) came from US Navy submarines and was adopted by Kaiser Permanente for nurses reporting to doctors; it delivers a patient crisis in under a minute, worst fact first, with the recommendation attached. (standing knowledge) Source: https://www.ihi.org/resources/tools/sbar-tool-situation-background-assessment-recommendation. Why it matters here: when a deploy breaks or a lane fails, the reply's last block should be the SBAR, not a narrative that gets to the red on line forty.

## WHAT THE BEST DO THAT WE DON'T

- They put the decision first and they put it where the eye lands. For Paolo that is the bottom. Our WHAT I NEED FROM YOU block is there by law, but the sentence above it is still often the proof line or a path, so his first read is jargon.
- They let one number carry the status. The Army, GOV.UK and Amazon all count: 25 words, 6 pages, 28 percent. We say "a lot shipped" and "mostly green." He cannot picture that and he has said so.
- They never report green without a threshold. Every RAG rescue plan says the same thing: define red in numbers. We report lane rows as done because a gate passed, and the watermelon research says 60 percent of those are rosy.
- They show the build and shut up. Pixar, Nintendo and Valve put the director in front of the thing. We write a paragraph about the thing and name the tab at the end. The tab name and the one changed pixel should be the first words of the line, not the last.
- They write for the slow, tired, phone reader by cutting, not by simplifying tone. NN/g says about 20 percent gets read. Our replies hit his screen at 500 to 900 words and the TLDR is honest, but the 700 words above it are the reason he scrolls past the ask.
- They deliver bad news in a fixed shape, worst fact first. We have no fixed shape; a broken deploy gets described in the middle of a status paragraph.

## THE CHANGES FOR THE COORDINATOR

1. Hard word cap, measured. Every reply to Paolo is at most 150 words from the first line to the play link, and the reply gate computes Flesch-Kincaid on those words and refuses anything over grade 8.0 or over 150 words. Test: the gate prints both numbers; a reply that fails does not send.
2. Bottom five lines carry the whole message. The last five lines before the play link are, in order: the one changed thing with its TAB name as the first two words; the one number that proves it; the one red (or "No red"); WHAT I NEED FROM YOU; the two-sentence bottom line. Test: cover everything above those five lines; if he would lose a decision, the reply is wrong.
3. No colour without a number. A lane may only be reported "done" or "green" with the measured number next to it (frames, pixels, seconds, count), and "red" is any miss of a threshold written in the round plan before the round started. Test: grep the reply for done/green/shipped; each must have a digit within the same sentence.
4. Bad news in SBAR shape at the bottom, always above the ask. Worst fact first in one sentence, cause in one, what I did in one, what I recommend in one. Test: a reply that mentions a failure anywhere else and not in this block fails the gate.
5. The reply template, 12 lines maximum, every reply, in this order:

   ROUND DONE. (one line: the number of lanes that shipped, the number that did not)
   TAB NAME: the one new thing to look at, in one sentence, with one number.
   TAB NAME: a second new thing, if there is one. Else delete this line.
   RED: the worst fact in one sentence, or "No red this round."
   CAUSE: one sentence. FIX: one sentence. (delete if no red)
   proof: paths and gate names, one line, machine-only
   **WHAT I NEED FROM YOU**
   1. (one word answers it) or "Nothing, I'm good."
   Bottom line: what I just did, one sentence.
   What you do with it and why it matters, one sentence.

## ONE RULE
THE LAST FIVE LINES ARE THE REPLY: write the decision, the one number, the one red, the ask and the two-sentence bottom line as the last five lines he sees, cap the whole thing at 150 words and grade 8, and treat every word above those lines as something he will not read.

## FOR PAOLO, IN PLAIN WORDS
The best bosses in the world get the answer first, in short words, with one number, and they get shown the thing instead of told about it. From now on every reply to you is under 150 words and the last five lines carry everything: what changed, which tab, what broke, what I need, and the bottom line. If you ever have to scroll up to find a decision, I did it wrong.
