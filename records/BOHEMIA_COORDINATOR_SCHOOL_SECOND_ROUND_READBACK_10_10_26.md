# BOHEMIA COORDINATOR SCHOOL, ROUND TWO OF TEN: READBACK (10/10/26)

## THE ANGLE
This round leaves games for the rooms where a misheard sentence kills somebody: the control tower, the operating theatre, the infantry radio net, the reactor control room, the railroad dispatch desk. Each learned the same lesson by burying people, and the lesson is not "speak more clearly." It is that an instruction does not exist until the receiver says it back and the sender confirms the readback. Three legs, not two. Our board runs on two: the coordinator writes a row, a lane claims it; Paolo says a sentence, the coordinator files it. The sounds misread lived for days because nobody had to say back what they heard, to the person who said it.

## WHAT THAT WORLD KNOWS
1. ICAO makes level, heading and speed instructions mandatory readback items, and the controller is required to listen to the readback and correct any error at once (Annex 11, 3.7.3). "Roger", "Wilco" and "Copied" are never allowed in place of a readback. https://skybrary.aero/articles/read-back-or-hear-back . Bites here: a lane's "claimed" commit is a Roger, receipt without understanding.
2. The FAA splits the duty in two. The pilot reads back altitudes, vectors and runways "in the same sequence as they are given" (AIM 4-4-7); the controller must "ensure that items read back are correct" (Order 7110.65, 2-4-3). https://www.angleofattack.com/aim/chapter-4/4-4-7/ and https://avweb.com/features/hear-back-read-back/ . Bites here: the hearback is the sender's job. The coordinator cannot hand it to the lane.
3. An FAA study of 51 hours of en route traffic found roughly 71% of pilot responses contained a faulty readback and 67% had an omission, omissions rising as the controller's message got longer. https://libraryonline.erau.edu/online-full-text/faa-aviation-medicine-reports/AM09-02.pdf . Bites here: a row with six clauses and three rule numbers is a long clearance; the dropped clause is the one nobody reports.
4. An ASRS review of 386 communication-error reports found altitude deviations were the most common result, and the top causes were similar call signs, pilot expectation, and controller workload. https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA356655.pdf . Bites here: the coordinator expected a hold, so it heard a hold.
5. Tenerife, 1977: 583 dead. The KLM first officer ended his readback with the home-made phrase "we are now at take-off"; the tower answered "OK, stand by for take-off" and a second transmission stepped on it. The recommendations stressed standard phraseology and the removal of the word "take-off" from departure clearances. https://skybrary.aero/accidents-and-incidents/b742-b741-tenerife-canary-islands-spain-1977 and https://aerocorner.com/blog/tenerife-1977/ . Bites here: one phrase, two honest readings; the fix was reserving the word.
6. Haynes et al., NEJM 2009: the WHO checklist across eight hospitals, 3,733 patients before and 3,955 after. Deaths fell from 1.5% to 0.8%, complications from 11.0% to 7.0%. The checklist is spoken aloud; each item is said by one person and confirmed by another. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2810011 . Bites here: the surgeons did not change; the saying-back did, and a third of the deaths went away.
7. TeamSTEPPS calls it the check-back, three steps exactly: sender initiates, receiver repeats back, sender verifies. If the restatement is wrong the sender clarifies and runs it again. https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/curriculum/communication/tools/checkback.html . Bites here: our board has step one and a half.
8. The Joint Commission's 2003 safety goal: a verbal or telephone order must be written down, read back in full, and confirmed by the person who gave it. https://psnet.ahrq.gov/web-mm/say-it-again . Bites here: Paolo's voice-to-text sentence is a telephone order. Right now it is written down and never read back.
9. Military radio bans "repeat" for "say again" because in ACP 125 REPEAT is an artillery proword meaning fire the same volume again. One word, two departments, and the wrong one gets shelled. https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Proword . Bites here: our two-word labels and rule numbers are prowords. "Section 13" was read backwards once already.
10. The Army's confirmation brief and back-brief: minutes after receiving the order, each subordinate tells the commander, in his own words, his task, his purpose, the intent and how he plans to do it. The reason is the title of the piece: "I don't know what I've said until you tell me what you've heard." https://chiefexecutive.net/i-dont-know-what-ive-said-until-you-tell-me-what-youve-heard/ and https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/library/report/call/call_91-1_sec3.htm . Bites here: a lane never says how it plans to do the row; a feature got built four times.
11. Nuclear plants and the power grid run three-way communication: sender states, receiver repeats the critical information, sender says "that is correct" or starts over; letters and numbers go phonetic. https://www.hpog.org/assets/documents/HPOG-3WAY-COMMUNICATIONS.pdf and https://www.nerc.com/pa/rrm/bpsa/Alerts%20DL/2009%20Advisories/A-2009-07-15-01_revised.pdf . Bites here: 22 s versus 48 s is two lanes measuring different things under one label, nothing repeated.
12. Railroads: 49 CFR 220.61 says a radioed track warrant must be repeated back and the dispatcher must verify it. In one FRA case the conductor repeated a wrong limit and the dispatcher verified it anyway. https://railroads.fra.dot.gov/sites/fra.dot.gov/files/fra_net/2195/EQ-12-05.pdf . Kitchens do the cheap version: a cook says "heard" or calls the ticket back, because a nod cannot be checked. https://www.louisvillehotbytes.com/?p=4055 . Bites here: a hearback that is only a glance stamps the error approved.

## WHAT THE COORDINATOR DOES NOT DO
- It never reads Paolo's sentence back to him before filing it as a rule. The sounds misread would have died in one line: "WHAT I HEARD: hold the sounds chat." He would have said "no."
- It never asks the lane to say the row back. A claim commit says "claimed," which is a Roger. "I read this as: how long the full suite runs" next to another lane's "I read this as: time to first frame" would have exposed the 22 s versus 48 s split on the first sweep.
- It does not hear back. It writes 26 notes from 200 commits and the next sweep checks whether work happened, not whether the work matched the row. The dispatcher who verified the wrong limit at least looked.
- It uses home-made phrases as if they were standard. "Hold", "s13", "two words per job" each carry two readings. Tenerife's fix was retiring the word that could be heard two ways, not better pilots.
- It lets long rows ride. The FAA number says omissions climb with message length. A five-clause row gets built with four.

## THE ONE CHANGE
Three-way on both ends of the coordinator, starting this hour, no new file, no new gate yet.

Leg one, the lane's readback. When a lane claims a row, the claim commit carries exactly one added line, in the lane's own words, never a paste of the row: `READBACK <row label>: I read this as: <one sentence saying what will be built and how it will be measured>; done means <one testable thing>.` Example: `READBACK PLUMBER [suite clock]: I read this as: time the full gate suite from the first gate's start to the last gate's exit, on the laptop, three runs averaged; done means a number in seconds on my handoff row.` Thirty words or fewer. If it cannot fit, the row was too long; the lane writes `SAY AGAIN <row label>: <the clause it cannot read>` and does not start.

Leg two, the coordinator's hearback. On the next sweep, before reading any other commit, the coordinator answers every READBACK since the last sweep with one of two words on the lane's board row: `CORRECT` or `NEGATIVE: <the one thing it got wrong, in a new sentence, not the old one>`. A NEGATIVE restarts the loop; the lane rewrites its READBACK before touching code. A claim with no readback is not a claim; the coordinator writes `NO READBACK, ROW OPEN` on it. The sweep's tally gains one line: `readbacks heard: N of N claims; negatives: N`.

Leg three, the readback to Paolo. Every time the coordinator turns a sentence of his into a rule or a job, the reply carries, directly above WHAT I NEED FROM YOU, one line: `WHAT I HEARD: <his instruction in one plain sentence, eighth grade, no label, no number>.` Never more than three per reply. He corrects with one word or ignores it; silence through his next VAMILY means CORRECT and the rule locks. A ruling filed without this line is not filed.

Reserved words: `READBACK`, `SAY AGAIN`, `CORRECT`, `NEGATIVE`, `WHAT I HEARD` mean only this and appear nowhere else on the board. "Hold" is retired; a lane is `FROZEN` (produces nothing) or it is working.

## ONE RULE
NOTHING IS HEARD UNTIL IT IS SAID BACK AND THE SENDER SAYS "CORRECT". A row, a ruling or a sentence from Paolo is not an instruction until the receiver has said it back in their own words and the one who said it has confirmed the readback.

## FOR PAOLO, IN PLAIN WORDS
Pilots, surgeons and reactor crews all repeat an order back before they act, and the person who gave it has to say "correct." We do not do that, which is how "make better sounds" turned into "stop the sounds chat." From now on every time I turn your words into a rule, I write one line that says what I heard, and you can kill it with one word.
