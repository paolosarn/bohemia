## 0. WHY SCHOOL COMES FIRST HERE, AND IT IS NOT A PROCEDURAL EXCUSE

The published practice has one line that settles it: **"you only truly get to see a player
experience your game for the first time once."** A walk taken before the sheet is written is a
walk spent inventing the sheet, and it is the only walk that can never be repeated. So this round
writes the sheet. Next round walks it and writes nothing else.

---

## 1. WHAT A WATCHER ACTUALLY RECORDS, AND IT IS THE CHARTER OF THIS LANE

The single most-repeated instruction in the observation literature:

> **Note what you OBSERVE, not what you INTERPRET.** *"Player spent 30 seconds looking around the
> room, opening the menu twice, before finding the door"* is an observation. *"Player was lost"*
> is an interpretation. Record the observation; interpret later, across sessions.

That is this lane's charter written by somebody else: **never decide taste, report the fact.**
So every line of THE STRANGER'S LIST is what a camera would have recorded, and never a verdict.

The rest of the practice, each one adopted below:
- **Timestamps on everything**, so a note can be tied back to the exact moment.
- **Silent observation.** The watcher sits back and does not help. When a player gets stuck, what
  they do to get unstuck is the finding.
- **A structured sheet prepared in advance**, with sections for first reactions, what they tried
  to do, and where they got stuck.
- And the one thing the standard sheet has that **this lane must not copy: "Overall Enjoyment,
  1 to 5."** A score is taste, taste is DIRECTION's, and the board row already says it: *never a
  score, one line a break.* The sheet below drops that row on purpose.

---

## 2. THE FIVE MINUTES IS AN INDUSTRY NUMBER, NOT JUST HIS NUMBER

The first-session literature lands on the same window he did, from the other side:

- **Most churn happens before the first day ends**, and players who hit technical issues, a
  confusing tutorial or a difficulty spike in their first session often never return.
- **Top performers keep the tutorial under 30 seconds** and let the player take a meaningful
  action immediately.
- A measured case: **giving players up to a minute to engage with no instruction produced
  confusion and churn**, because they did not know the goal.
- And the guidance says outright: **put an exciting action sequence in the first five minutes**,
  to show what the game can become.

His *"I have not experienced any combat yet"* is that rule, failed. His *"nothing happens"* is
the minute-without-a-goal, failed. The five minutes he chose is the window the trade already
measures.

So the walk carries **three stopwatch numbers**, and they are the only numbers on the sheet:
**time to first input, time to first meaningful action, time to first fight.**

---

## 3. HIS WORST BUG HAS A PUBLISHED NAME, AND THE LAW'S RANKING IS CORRECT

*"It says a car is gonna pull up on me and then nothing happens."* The law ranks that above a
missing feature. The research agrees and names it: a **FALSE AFFORDANCE** -- an element whose
appearance promises an interaction that does not exist; a dead click; *"a small broken promise,
where the perceived affordance wrote a cheque the real affordance would not cash."*

And the finding that justifies the ranking:

> **Each encounter with a false affordance erodes the user's trust in the ENTIRE system.** If an
> element promises interactivity and fails, the user begins to doubt **all** similar visual
> components, increasing cognitive load and the sense of insecurity.

That is the mechanism behind *"nothing's complete"* from one dead card. A missing feature costs
you the feature. A dead promise costs you every other promise on the screen. **So on the sheet,
a dead affordance is written down first, above anything cosmetic**, and it is the one class of
break that is counted rather than merely listed.

---

## 4. WHAT THE STANDARD PROTOCOL CANNOT GIVE US, SAID PLAINLY

**(a) I AM NOT A STRANGER, AND NO PROTOCOL CAN MAKE ME ONE.** Every published five-minute
protocol rests on a first-time player. I have read this build's source for twenty-five rounds; I
know where the buttons are and what they do. **THE STRANGER'S LIST may therefore never claim
naivety.** It records only what a camera would record, which is the one kind of note a
non-naive watcher can still take honestly. Where a genuinely naive reaction is the evidence
needed, the list says so and routes it to a real stranger -- the demo-in-hands law already
exists for putting it in a friend's hands.

**(b) THE WATCHER AND THE PLAYER ARE THE SAME PROCESS HERE**, which the literature treats as a
bias to design around (hence moderators segregating themselves from the game). The mitigation is
the **tap script fixed in advance**, below: a walk that decides its own route while walking will
drift toward the parts that work.

**(c) A COLD FIRST RUN, EVERY TIME.** Fresh storage, no save, the plain link, phone size. The
literature's whole value comes from the first-time state, and this build persists a save; a walk
on a warm save is not the five minutes anybody else gets.

**(d) THE BREAK LIST IS NOT THE SHEET.** RUN posts THE BREAK LIST, in his words' order. This lane
posts THE STRANGER'S LIST, in the order things went wrong on the walk. They will overlap and
that is the point: **the overlap is the control** (section 6).

## 5. THE SHEET, WHICH IS WHAT ROUND TWO FILLS IN AND NOTHING ELSE

**THE COLD START.** Fresh context, no stored save, `slices/BOHEMIA_DEMO.html` at 390x844, DPR 2,
touch on. Page errors and stalls recorded as observations (PLUMBER owns fixing them; this lane
only writes down that they happened, with the timestamp).

**THE TAP SCRIPT, DECIDED NOW SO THE WALK CANNOT STEER ITSELF.** In this order, and no other:
1. the front splash: read what it says, tap to enter;
2. whatever is largest and most obviously tappable on the first screen, first;
3. walk in one direction until something stops you;
4. every card or offer that appears, in the order it appears, tapped once;
5. the map, and from the map, whatever claims to take you somewhere;
6. the zoom, out and back in;
7. stop at five minutes exactly, mid-action if necessary.

**EACH LINE OF THE LIST HAS EXACTLY FOUR PARTS:** `mm:ss | what I did | what happened on screen |
what a stranger was promised`. No adjectives, no verdict, no score.

**THE THREE NUMBERS:** time to first input, time to first meaningful action, time to first fight
(or NONE, with the elapsed time at which the walk ended).

**THE ONE COUNT:** dead affordances met -- things that look tappable and do nothing, or promise a
thing that does not happen. Counted because section 3 says they are load-bearing for trust.

**AND WHAT IS NOT ON THE SHEET:** a score, a ranking, "this looks bad", a gate count, a commit
count. Rule 14(e) bans the last two in an answer to him, and this lane's charter bans the first
three.

## 6. RULE ZERO FOR A WALK, WHICH IS THE HARD PART

A subjective walk has no obvious positive control. Here is one, and it is the reason the overlap
with RUN's list matters:

> **THE KNOWN-BREAK CONTROL.** He named seven breaks and RUN posts them as THE BREAK LIST. Those
> breaks are known to be present. **If the walk does not independently surface the ones that fall
> inside its five minutes, the protocol is not sensitive enough to be trusted on anything new.**
> A sheet that misses a break he personally hit is a broken instrument, not a clean bill.

Plus three cheap ones:
- **the cold-start control:** the walk must begin with no stored save; if a save is found, the
  run is void and restarted, because a warm start is not the five minutes.
- **the clock control:** the stopwatch is the page's own monotonic clock, not wall time. E14
  measured the two clocks in this build 9.6 ms apart with 0.93 ms of jitter; a five-minute budget
  does not care, but a "time to first input" of 0.4 s does.
- **the error control:** a page error deliberately raised must be captured by the recorder, or the
  recorder cannot claim zero errors. E12's whole lesson was that a zero needs a positive control.

**And one thing this round will not pretend.** Reading the shipped demo file, the literal strings
he named do not both appear in it: "DROP IN" occurs once and "PRETTY MAP" does not occur at all.
That does **not** mean the buttons are gone -- labels can be built at runtime, and the file on
disk is not the file he played. **Whether those two buttons are on screen is a round-two
observation, not a round-one claim**, and it is written here so that next round cannot quietly
decide it either way.
