## 1. RAILWAY MANIA, BRITAIN, THE 1840s
Hundreds of competing railway companies were floated on pure speculation; share prices for
companies that had not laid a single mile of track still soared. The crash (mid-to-late 1840s)
wiped out most of the paper value and the smaller, undercapitalized companies, including
projects that never finished. WHAT SURVIVED: the physical track, cuttings, tunnels, bridges and
stations that WERE actually built -- once the paper companies failed, the real infrastructure
did not disappear, it got consolidated. Surviving and larger companies absorbed the bankrupt
smaller ones' completed lines at a fraction of their original construction cost, which is how
Britain ended up with a dense, genuinely useful rail network built mostly during a period that
investors experienced as a catastrophic loss. THE PATTERN: the money that built it was
destroyed; the rails themselves were not, and ended up owned by whoever was still solvent when
the music stopped.

## 2. THE TELECOM BUBBLE, ~2000
Carriers borrowed heavily (often financed directly by their own equipment vendors, Lucent and
Nortel extending credit so carriers could buy the gear to build out networks neither side could
actually pay for) to lay enormous amounts of fiber-optic cable, betting on an explosion in
internet traffic that took years longer to arrive than the financing assumed. When the bubble
burst, a large share of the fiber laid in this period was never even connected to equipment and
sat dark in the ground -- real estimates from the period put the unlit fraction of laid fiber
at a large majority of total mileage. WHAT SURVIVED: the physical cable and conduit itself,
buried and largely undamaged by the companies' bankruptcy. WHO GOT IT FOR PENNIES: bankrupt
carriers (Global Crossing among the most cited) had their physical fiber networks bought out of
bankruptcy court by new owners and private investors at a tiny fraction of the original
build cost, and that "dark fiber" became the cheap backbone a later generation of internet
companies lit up and used for a fraction of what it cost to lay.

## 3. THE DOT-COM CRASH, ~2000-2001
Startups burned venture money building warehouses, logistics networks and office space on the
assumption that explosive growth would arrive before the cash ran out; most did not reach
profitability and folded within a year or two of the peak. WHAT SURVIVED: the physical
buildings (warehouses, fulfillment centers, office campuses) and, less obviously, the
EXCESS SERVER AND DATA-CENTER CAPACITY bought to handle growth that never came -- the same
shape as the telecom story one layer up the stack, computing capacity instead of cable. WHO GOT
IT FOR PENNIES: landlords and real-estate buyers picked up vacated office space and warehouses
at post-crash prices, and the surplus server infrastructure a failed company had already paid
for became cheap or free capacity for whoever survived -- one of the most durable real patterns
in business history is that the SURVIVOR of a tech bubble often runs on hardware a DEAD company
already paid to build.

## 4. THE 2008 CRASH, LAS VEGAS ITSELF
This is the one with no translation needed at all, because the valley this game is set in is
the real place it happened. Las Vegas was one of the hardest-hit housing markets in the entire
country: half-finished condo towers and subdivisions sat abandoned for years (the stalled
Fontainebleau Las Vegas tower is the most cited single example, a nearly-complete skyscraper
left standing empty for most of a decade), and whole platted subdivisions on the city's edge
were left as graded dirt and empty cul-de-sacs, never built at all. WHAT SURVIVED: the concrete
skeletons, the graded and plotted land, and the empty infrastructure (roads, utility stubs)
already run out to lots nobody ever built on. WHO GOT IT FOR PENNIES: large institutional
buyers, most famously Blackstone, bought tens of thousands of foreclosed single-family homes
in bulk across the hardest-hit markets (Las Vegas and Phoenix among the most cited) directly
out of foreclosure auctions, converting them into rental portfolios at a fraction of their
pre-crash price -- the exact same pattern as the other three, a crash destroys the FINANCE and
leaves the PHYSICAL THING to be picked up cheap by whoever still has cash.

## 5. THE PATTERN, STATED ONCE
Every one of these four crashes is the same shape: speculative financing builds something real
and physical faster than the real economy can use it; the finance collapses; the physical thing
does not vanish, it changes hands at a steep discount to whoever has cash left standing. That
is the exact shape of a world forty years after a currency collapse: nobody doubts the valley's
buildings and cables are real, they doubt whether anyone still owns them in a way that can be
enforced.
