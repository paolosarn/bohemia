## 1. THE ANSWER FIRST
Of the eighty cells (twenty fields, four studios), 51 are a studio's own statement, 2 are secondary (a journalist or a developer-adjacent list), 1 are a community measurement, 4 are my inference marked as such, and 22 are NOT PUBLISHED by anyone. So the honest shape of this page is: the four studios publish the top of the guide (identity, resolution, camera, how the light works, how a frame is made, what wins when rules fight) and almost none of the bottom (outline rule, dither rule, variation counts, contact line, do-and-don't pairs). The bottom is what a studio keeps in its head and its files. DIRECTION will not find those numbers in anybody's blog; it has to SET them and let the gates hold them, which is exactly what rule 114a says.

What the four agree on, where they do publish:
- ONE SIZE, DECLARED. The two pixel-art games with a published number both chose 640x360 (Blasphemous from the studio, Dead Cells from a developer-adjacent list); Eastward's players measure 360p; Battle Brothers chose no pixel grid at all, one painted pixel per screen pixel. Nobody runs two native pixel sizes in one scene. That is tell 1 of rule 114 and it is unanimous.
- THE LOOK IS DECIDED AT THE SOURCE, NOT ON THE SCREENSHOT. Dead Cells renders a 3D model small with no anti-aliasing and lights it through a hand-drawn normal map; Eastward splits every sprite by its structure and paints a bump map per part; Battle Brothers paints rough at 500x500 and judges at 48x48; Blasphemous places every pixel by hand. Four different sources, one rule: the pixel is authored, never filtered.
- THE LIGHT IS EITHER BAKED OR DYNAMIC, NEVER BOTH. Blasphemous and Battle Brothers bake it into the paint (no runtime light on the sprite). Dead Cells and Eastward run real lights over normal or bump maps and tint whole levels with them. There is no middle: a sprite with a painted key light under a dynamic light is the mixel of lighting.
- READABILITY AT THE SMALLEST SIZE IS THE TIE-BREAKER, SAID OUT LOUD by the only one of the four that wrote it down (Battle Brothers: faces readable at the most zoomed-out level, weapons out of proportion so they read). Dead Cells' tie-breaker is speed, Blasphemous' is purity, Eastward's is the light.
- A NAMED DEFECT EACH. Dead Cells: flickering pixels. Battle Brothers: noisy downscaled detail. Both name ONE, not eleven. The guide's list of eleven mistakes (field 17) is longer than any studio's.

## 3. THE FINDING THAT PROVES US WRONG, MEASURED AGAINST OUR REPO (rule 6: one per round)
FIELD 5, THE COLOUR CAP PER ASSET CLASS, IS NOT A THING THREE OF THE FOUR STUDIOS DO, AND OUR GATE FOR IT WOULD FAIL THE LOOK HE LOCKED.
- Rule 114a's Section C example caps colours per sprite (character 12, prop 6, tile 5, portrait 24), and rule 114b's gate 2 (palette lock and colour count) is to run "over the PNGs a VOTE sheet carries and over the demo's screenshots", with a sheet that fails the first five gates never reaching the tab.
- Battle Brothers is painted raster with thousands of colours per bust and no cap anywhere. Dead Cells ships each frame with a normal map and lets a toon shader and dynamic lights decide the on-screen colour, so a count on a screenshot is a count of the torches. Eastward does the same with deferred lights plus a LUT grade on top. Only Blasphemous, hand-placed and unlit, is a game where a per-sprite colour count on screen means what the gate thinks it means.
- Ours is the Dead Cells case, by his ruling. Rule 115 (10/17, LOCKED) makes the rig the source: a model, clay shading, one hard light, rendered to pixels. DIRECTION's own round three measured the rig's renders at 10,146 and 13,047 colours per sheet and wrote "his art is not limited-palette; fix the source, not the output", and that is the look he said he fell in love with. A 12-colour cap measured on the render or the screenshot rejects every one of those sheets.
- So field 5 cannot be a count on the output. It has to be a count on the SOURCE: the master palette file's roles (field 4) that the rig snaps toward (the round-three population snap, 0.6 of the way toward the pack family), measured on the material colours before the light, with the on-screen count left free. Dead Cells' "keeping its base colors" under a torch is the same split: base colours are a rule, lit colours are not.
- What this changes: rule 114b's gate 2 needs two legs (a palette-lock leg on the source materials and the pack tiles, which are hand-placed and can be capped; no colour-count leg on a lit render or a screenshot), or it culls the rig before he sees it. Routed to DIRECTION (the field) and PLUMBER (the gate) below. Nothing is built by this lane.

## 4. THE REAL WORLD (the second angle, rule 6): HOW A STUDIO LOCKS A LOOK BEFORE A FRAME IS SHOT
- A 2D animation studio locks colours in a MASTER PALETTE per character, prop and effect; the paint tool refuses a colour outside it, and editing one swatch repaints every zone that used it (Toon Boom Harmony's palette model, the industry tool). That is field 4 as a machine gate, in use for decades: the cap is on the palette file, the artist cannot leave it, and the count is never taken on the final composite with the lights and effects on.
- The MODEL SHEET fixes proportions and which colour goes where, per character, from several angles, before animation starts (field 10 and field 4 as one document).
- The COLOUR SCRIPT fixes the palette per sequence as a board of key frames with the colours sketched over them, before production; the big studios' versions are far more detailed than an independent's (field 4 and field 20 as one board, in time order).
- Stop motion, the rig's own real-world twin (rule 115): Aardman processes hundreds of pounds of clay several ways so that a character's colour and consistency stay the same across a shoot, "the same shade at frame 6800 as it did at frame 1". The palette is a material rule at the source, not a count on the photograph; the light on set is held constant so continuity holds. Same lesson as section 3, from the real world.

## 5. WHAT DIRECTION READS BEFORE IT PICKS, FIELD BY FIELD (the short form; the table is the long form)
- 2 (pixel size): declare ONE internal canvas and integer scale; 640x360 is the published pick of the two pixel-art games here; Battle Brothers' 1:1 painting is the other road and it is not ours (rule 114, tell 1). Our person is one size everywhere already (the ground may zoom law); the canvas is the missing number.
- 4 and 5 (palette, cap): a master palette FILE with roles, capped at the source; the cap never measured on a lit render (section 3).
- 6 (light): pick baked OR dynamic for the fight board and the people, never both on one screen; the rig is one hard light baked into the render, so the board's tiles must carry the same baked light and no runtime light sits on top.
- 7 and 9 (shading, AA): Dead Cells is the published twin of our pipe (3D, small, no AA, toon steps, hand cleanup where worth it) and it names the leftover defect, flickering pixels; our round-three fault list already has grain and airbrush, so flicker across frames is the one to add to field 17.
- 15 (timing): keys first with the fewest frames that convince, then in-betweens (Dead Cells); ours is 12 keys a bar at 120 BPM and the envelope law; the published practice agrees with drawing fewer keys well.
- 18 (tie-breaker): readability at the smallest size is the only tie-breaker a studio here wrote down; keep it first.
- 8, 11, 12, 13, 16, 17 (outline, detail budget, variation, contact, pairs, mistakes): nobody publishes these; they are ours to set and the gates to hold. The one published hint for 11 is Battle Brothers': paint at the size it is judged at, because downscaled detail is noise.
