# WORDS Q10 -- THREATS THAT DE-ESCALATE AND THREATS THAT ESCALATE
# VAMILY research round, 9/5/26, lane WORDS (words-8dqrnq). MODE: RESEARCH.
# The question, verbatim: "Threats that de-escalate and threats that escalate.
# What real negotiators and the best-written standoffs do with a sentence, for
# the gambit orders (day 3)."

## THE ANSWER IN ONE LINE
**A threat is not a strong sentence, it is the LAST rung before a fight, and our
city has built every rung except that one. We can order you and we can warn you.
Nobody in 1,669 lines can threaten you, insult you, or say anything at all inside
a fight.**

## 1. THE FINDING THAT PROVES US WRONG
I expected to find our dialogue too soft and to prescribe threats. Both halves
were wrong.

Measured across 1,669 NPC-spoken lines in 45 sources, every hit read by hand:

    RUNG                              what the build has
    1  identity attack on you                  0
    2  order (bare imperative at you)        242   (14.5% of lines)
    3  first-person threat ("I'll ...")        0
    3b warning (consequence, not from me)      5   in 5 different quests
    4  a word spoken in a fight                0   (0 combat sources of 45)

Two things fall out of that table and neither is what I went looking for.

**FIRST: THE WARNING FORM IS THE CORRECT ONE AND WE ALREADY DO IT.** Schelling
defines a threat as "some voluntary but irreversible sacrifice of freedom of
choice": to threaten is to tie your OWN hands. A warning costs nothing because it
just reveals what was already going to happen, which is why it is more credible.
The negotiation literature states the goal outright: **turn your threats into
warnings.** Our writers found that by instinct.

**SECOND, AND THIS IS THE DEFECT: WE FOUND IT ONCE AND THEN SAID IT FIVE TIMES.**

    S05  "That isn't a threat. It's just the truest thing I know."
    S10  "That isn't a threat, that's arithmetic."
    S17  "That isn't a threat. That's just how it goes."
    S26  "I'm not threatening you. I'm telling you the arithmetic."
    bark "That's not cruelty, that's arithmetic."

Five speakers, five separate quests, one rhetorical move, and the word
**arithmetic in three of the five.** The whole valley applies pressure the same
way and then announces that it is not applying pressure. **The voice gate cannot
see this.** It is not a repeated opener and it is not a banned phrase, so 111
green checks pass over it. That is the round's real finding: not that we are too
soft, but that we have exactly ONE pressure move and it has a catchphrase.

## 2. THE STAIRCASE IS REAL AND IT IS MEASURED
Felson and Steadman, 159 homicide and assault incidents, coded turn by turn. The
order is stable: **identity attack, then a failed attempt to influence, then a
threat, then the attack.** Retaliation is the engine: an aggressive move by one
side predicts an aggressive move by the other and predicts who dies.

Three things follow that a writer can use:

1. **A THREAT IS LATE, NOT EARLY.** It is the sentence just before the fight. A
   character who opens with one is not menacing, he is out of order, and the
   scene reads as a cartoon.
2. **AN ORDER IS RUNG 2.** Telling somebody what to do IS the influence attempt.
   We have 242 of those and no rung above or below them.
3. **THE THIRD PARTY DECIDES.** Studies of filmed real fights find third parties
   mirror the antagonists: a calm intervention lowers the temperature, an
   aggressive one raises it. **We have an automated companion in every fight.
   That companion is the third party, and right now it is silent.**

## 3. THE AUDIENCE IS WHY OUR GAME ESCALATES BY DEFAULT
Felson's other line of work is aggression as impression management: most violence
is about identity in front of somebody. **Backing down in private costs nothing.
Backing down in front of a crowd costs your standing, so people fight instead.**

Our combat is ALWAYS against a group (9/4). **Every standoff in Bohemia has an
audience by construction**, which means the default temperature of our fights is
high and no writer chose that.

We already have the one line in the build that understands it, and it is the best
threat we own:

    sergeant: "Ask me that again in front of my crew and you'll find out what we're
               actually like. Ask me here and I'll tell you it keeps me up."

That is the entire research finding in two sentences: **same question, two
temperatures, and the only variable is who is listening.**

## 4. WHAT ACTUALLY DE-ESCALATES, AND IT IS NOT A BETTER THREAT
- **VOICE.** The procedural-justice components are respect, neutrality, voice and
  trustworthiness. Voice means the other person gets to talk. A US city
  randomised trial of that training measured **10% fewer complaints and officers
  58% less likely to injure somebody.** For a writer that is expensive: it costs a
  whole turn where your character says nothing and lets the other one finish.
- **THE GOLDEN BRIDGE** (Ury). **Many people will reject a deal rather than lose
  face.** So the de-escalating line does not offer a better deal, it offers a way
  to back down that does not look like backing down.
- **PARAPHRASE, NOT ARGUMENT.** Saying their own position back to them, accurately,
  is the single most-taught negotiator move.
- **SLOW IT DOWN.** Patience is itself the tool; the deadline is what makes a
  demand a threat. Remove the clock and the same sentence stops escalating. We
  have only two lines that put a clock on the listener, so this is a lever we have
  barely used in either direction.
- One caution, honestly labelled: a 2026 study of frontier models in simulated
  nuclear crises coded 285 conditional threats and found only **77 (27%) produced
  de-escalation** on the opponent's next turn, 208 held or escalated. That is
  simulated agents, not people, so it is not evidence about humans. It is
  suggestive about the FORM: a conditional threat mostly does not work.

## 5. THE THING THAT IS OURS ALONE, AND WE WELDED IT SHUT
Communication Accommodation Theory: **convergence** (moving your speech toward
the other person) reduces distance and puts people at ease; **divergence** (moving
away) is used to make somebody feel unwelcome. Divergence runs from partial, a few
words in the other language, to full, a whole different language. Separately, the
bilingual emotion work finds people switch **into L1 to turn emotion up** and into
L2 to turn it down.

**So in a bilingual city the temperature of a standoff is carried by the LANGUAGE
MIX, and it does not need a single threatening word.**

We are a Spanglish city. Every bark already ships in en, spanglish and es. And
measured in the engine:

    engine/bohemia_people.js  langOf(blockSeed, key)

**A person's register is a hash of who they are and where they live. It is fixed
at birth and nothing in the game can move it inside a conversation.** We built the
dial, we shipped all three positions of it, and we welded it to a person's ID.

That is the cheapest escalation system any game could have and no other game can
have it, because no other game is this city.

**AND IT MUST NOT BREAK THE CAP.** The language law (8/26) makes proportional the
ceiling, not a target. So a switch is a SWAP, never an addition: heat moves a
line's register one step and cool moves it one step back, and the valley's totals
do not move. That is a mechanic, so it is PEOPLE's to build, not mine to decide.

## 6. WHAT THE BEST GAMES DO
- **Alpha Protocol** is the closest fit to gambit orders: three standing stances,
  suave, professional and aggressive, chosen under a timer, and different people
  answer to different ones. The lesson is that **aggression is not a worse option,
  it is a key that fits some locks.**
- **Deus Ex: Human Revolution** reads the other person's type (alpha, beta, omega)
  and asks you to match your pressure to it. The player is not picking "nice or
  mean", they are **reading a person**, which is exactly the skill the day 3 study
  wants: reading the enemy's intent is the same skill as ordering your own.
- **Disco Elysium** makes failure the good outcome. A failed Authority check is
  funnier and more revealing than a passed one, which is the antidote to writing a
  threat as a coin flip that either "works" or wastes the player's turn.

## 7. THE SPEC FOR THE GAMBIT ORDERS
The day 3 study fixed the order set as INTENTS, a handful of words, one system for
everybody including the enemy: go, hold, slow down, fall back, get behind them,
stay with me. Q4 fixed the budget: **one beat at 120 BPM is about two words**, so
an order is two or three words or it misses its beat.

That means the order words are already correct and are ALSO rung 2 of the
staircase. One vocabulary, two jobs. What is missing is the temperature:

    THE SAME INTENT, THREE TEMPERATURES
    intent        cool (converge, give voice)      flat (the order)   hot (diverge, remove the choice)
    hold          "give it a second"               "hold"             "nobody moves"
    fall back     "we're done here"                "fall back"        "out. now."
    get behind    "go around, I'll keep him"       "get behind them"  "cut him off"
    stay with me  "stay where I can see you"       "stay with me"     "on me"

**AND THE FOUR RULES THAT COME OUT OF THE RESEARCH:**
1. **THE COMPANION SPEAKS FIRST AND SETS THE TEMPERATURE.** It is the third party,
   and the third party mirrors. One line from it before the first swing.
2. **A THREAT IS THE LAST LINE BEFORE THE FIGHT, NEVER THE FIRST.** If the enemy
   threatens, the fight is already happening.
3. **THE WARNING FORM STAYS, BUT IT LOSES ITS CATCHPHRASE.** Nobody says "that
   isn't a threat" ever again. The form is right, the tell must die.
4. **THE WAY OUT IS ALWAYS ON THE TABLE AND IT IS ALWAYS PRIVATE.** In front of the
   group there is no de-escalating sentence, because backing down in public is the
   thing people fight to avoid. The de-escalating line is the one that gets the
   other person off the stage.
