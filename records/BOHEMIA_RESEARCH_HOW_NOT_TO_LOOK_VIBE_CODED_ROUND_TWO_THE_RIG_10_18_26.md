**The question for this round.** Since round one the creative director ruled that every character, beast, vehicle and prop is MODELLED IN 3D (Blender, headless) and RENDERED DOWN TO PIXEL SPRITES at eight facings through one rig, in a clay-and-tape, analog-horror, creepy-claymation-kids-show look. The robot and the truck made this way are the only art he has loved. His standing demand is "I need you to know how to make things that don't look vibecoded." So: how do the studios that render 3D to 2D pixel sprites keep the result from looking like a cheap render, and what is the hand-finish?

## SECTION 0: WHAT THE STUDIOS ACTUALLY DO (the evidence base, short)

**Dead Cells (Motion Twin, 2017 to 2018 write-ups).** Thomas Vasseur, the only artist for the first year, built a 2D model sheet, then a 3D character and skeleton in 3DS Max. The character is about 50 pixels tall in game, so a heavy 3D model "does not seem to be particularly effective"; the model is cheap and the time goes elsewhere. A "little homebrew program, developed for this very specific task, will then render the mesh in a very small size and without antialiasing, giving us that pixelated look." Each frame is exported as a PNG plus a normal map, "this allows us to render the volume using the simplest toon shader," which the community reads as "a typical 2 tone cell shading." Animations hit 30 fps and are keyframed like 2D, with in-betweens added after the keys. Backgrounds are a different pipeline: hand-painted, then "redrawn in normal maps" so the dynamic light can tint them. The custom renderer was never released. Sources: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/art-design-deep-dive-using-a-3d-pipeline-for-2d-animation-in-i-dead-cells-i- (2018, excerpt) ; https://www.gameanim.com/2018/01/31/dead-cells-3d-pipeline-2d-animation/ (2018) ; https://sudonull.com/post/14066-Dead-Cells-Using-3D-Pipeline-for-2D-Animation (2018, translation) ; https://habr.com/en/articles/416589/ (2018) ; https://80.lv/articles/interview-with-the-developers-of-dead-cells (2017) ; https://discussions.unity.com/t/the-visual-effects-of-dead-cells/689349 (2018, secondhand on the 2-tone read) ; https://github.com/ProtoArt/spritec (2019, "a custom private tool that was never released").
What this means for us: Dead Cells did NOT bake the light. It shipped a flat-colour sprite plus a normal map and lit it at runtime with a two-step toon shader. The pixel look comes from three source settings (tiny render size, no anti-aliasing, a stepped shader), not from a cleanup pass. The debate on whether the result looks "rendered" is real: a Hacker News thread opens with "at first I thought the approach to dynamic lighting was overdone" (https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=18357749, 2018). That is the one tell their pipeline leaves in: runtime lighting on a flat sprite reads as plastic to some eyes.

**Ion Fury (Voidpoint, 3D Realms dev blog, 2018).** The documented hand-finish. Enemies are built as 3D models, but "the models are not the end result," they "focus on overall form and omit small details." The artist "picks specific animation frames and renders them out in all angles," then in Photoshop "cleans them up for a crisper, more pixel art look, restores lost details, polishes the sprites and adds stylisation." The stated reason: "downscaling the large renders to the target resolution causes many small details to disappear." Source: https://archive.3drealms.com/devblog/dev-blog-2-art-ion-maiden/ (2018). This is the closest published description of what Section B formalises.

**Diablo II (Blizzard North postmortem, 2000).** Almost all in-game art was built and rendered in 3D Studio Max; an in-house tool rendered models "from many angles, eight for monsters and 16 for player characters" and exported to the game formats, at 640x480 in 8-bit colour. Forum recollection (secondhand) adds that each file carried its own 256-colour palette and that body, armour and weapon were rendered as separate layers and composited at runtime. Sources: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/postmortem-blizzard-s-i-diablo-ii-i- (2000) ; https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/487300-question-about-diablo-2s-sprites/ (n.d., secondhand). The eight-facings-through-one-tool pattern is exactly our rig, 26 years old.

**Donkey Kong Country (Rare, 1994; retrospectives 2014).** SGI workstations rendered the models and a compression step turned them into SNES sprites; the lead programmer called reducing frame counts the hard part; a retro writer notes "the transfer of the render to a 16 colored sprite was a hard problem" because a render's "shaded corner is blending evenly, and you could not do that with this limit." A Rare staffer: "everything was rendered, even the numbers that showed the score; there wasn't a single thing that was hand-drawn." Sources: https://www.nintendolife.com/news/2014/02/month_of_kong_the_making_of_donkey_kong_country (2014) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Sutherland_(programmer) (2026) ; https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/1151506921754517506.html (2019, secondhand). Lesson: the palette limit is where a render stops looking like a render, and it is a quantisation problem before it is a drawing problem.

**Blasphemous (The Game Kitchen, for contrast).** Hand-drawn, at a fixed "virtual frame" of 360 pixels high with width set by the monitor (640x360 at 16:9). The art director began in UI and learned pixel art on the job because the team had no artists; animator Raul Vivar's time-lapses show new frames made "by manipulating current ones for rapid iteration." Sources: https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/828401966/blasphemous-dark-and-brutal-2d-non-linear-platform/posts/1901663 (2017) ; https://www.pcgamingwiki.com/wiki/Blasphemous (2026) ; https://www.pczone.co.uk/interview-blasphemous-2-art-director-enrique-cabeza/ (2023) ; https://www.gameanim.com/2021/02/19/blasphemous-pixel-art-animation-time-lapses/ (2021). The detail budget point: Blasphemous spends its hand on every frame of a 360-high screen, which is why it reads richer than Dead Cells frame for frame; Dead Cells spends its hand on the model and the keys and lets the machine do the in-betweens. We are a 60-boss, three-generation, phone game with a small team. We are Dead Cells plus Ion Fury's cleanup on the frames that matter, not Blasphemous.

**Octopath Traveler (Acquire and Square Enix, 2018 to 2023).** Pixel sprites billboarded in a 3D set, built in Unreal Engine 4. The lighting lesson is a single sentence from the Unreal spotlight: the team "first added normal visual effects for the combat sequences but it didn't look good enough, so they added a point light simultaneously with the visual effects," so the light "could create shadows of the characters on the environment." A developer rebuilding the style infers "invisible 3D shapes to cast shadows" behind the flat sprite (secondhand). Sources: https://www.unrealengine.com/spotlights/octopath-traveler-s-hd-2d-art-style-and-story-make-for-a-jrpg-dream-come-true (2018) ; https://www.unrealengine.com/en-US/developer-interviews/octopath-traveler-ii-builds-a-bigger-bolder-world-in-its-stunning-hd-2d-style (2023) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HD-2D (2026) ; https://dev.to/gaurav_de/creating-an-hd-2d-rendering-pipeline-on-dx12-205k (n.d., secondhand). The sprite itself stays hand-pixel and flat; the 3D does the shadow on the ground and the bloom and tilt-shift on the set. That is a hybrid where the pixel art is never lit per pixel, which is why it never reads as plastic.

**Sea of Stars (Sabotage Studio, 2023).** The press kit promises "full-on dynamic lighting" from "a custom-made render pipeline." Reviewers note the lighting "blends into the art rather than looking like a 3D effect overlaid on low-res 2D assets," a charged attack throws shadows from the other party members, and shadows, particles and warps "render at the same pixel-scale resolution." No developer talk on the method was found. Sources: https://sabotagestudio.com/presskits/sea-of-stars/ (2023) ; https://seasonedgaming.com/2023/08/28/review-sea-of-stars-more-than-a-tribute/ (2023) ; https://forum.quartertothree.com/t/sea-of-stars-retro-rpg-inspired-by-chrono-trigger/159690/19 (2023). The transferable rule is in the reviewer's sentence: every lighting effect lands on the same pixel grid as the sprite. A smooth light on a chunky sprite is the mixel of lighting (round one tell 1).

**Hyper Light Drifter (Heart Machine, 2014 to 2016).** "Our game is 480p... and very intentionally so," about the art's resolution, not the display's. Preston started "in a full HD, illustrated style," judged it too much for one person and moved to "a small resolution, a limited canvas." Environments are "big sections of flat colour then small details etched on top"; the glow is "soft lights on top of traditional colour limited pixel art." Sources: https://gamingbolt.com/our-game-is-480p-yeah-its-low-resolution-and-very-intentionally-so (2014) ; https://www.nintendolife.com/news/2014/11/resolution_doesnt_matter_with_2d_games_says_hyper_light_drifter_developer (2014) ; https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/road-to-the-igf-heart-machine-s-i-hyper-light-drifter-i- (2016) ; https://medium.com/the-space-ape-games-experience/hyper-light-drifter-ui-breakdown-c2d9cfe0a192 (n.d.) ; https://steamcommunity.com/app/257850/discussions/0/357284767236620083 (n.d., secondhand). No formal style guide was found. The consistency rule that is documented is the one canvas: one native resolution for all art, chosen for the team's size, and light as a soft layer on top of a colour-limited sprite rather than inside it.

**Blender Studio, "Exploring 3D Pixel Art in Blender 4.2" (2024).** The one first-party Blender source. Its finding that matters most to us: "From the compositor, I could add the pixelate filter, but this also included anti-aliasing. This would soften the edges and interpolate pixels, which is not ideal for pixel art," and an option to disable that was being discussed with developers. Source: https://studio.blender.org/blog/3d-pixel-art-in-blender/ (2024, excerpt). The manual's Pixelate node has a Pixel Size (renamed Size in 5.0) and only a Color input and output, no anti-alias toggle in the pages found: https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/4.1/compositing/types/filter/pixelate.html (2024) ; https://docs.blender.org/manual/ko/5.0/compositing/types/creative/pixelate.html (2026). Conclusion: do not make the final sprite with the Pixelate node. Render at the sprite's native size, or render at an integer multiple and downscale with Nearest or a k-centroid downscaler.

**t3ssel8r's look, as rebuilt by David Holland (Godot) and others.** The two source-side fixes for render tells: (1) "Snap the camera to a view-aligned, texel-sized grid. This removes pixel creep, but movement is no longer smooth. Take the render output and shift it back in screen space by the snap error." (2) Outlines from passes, not from the colour image: "To make the outlines a single pixel thick, I used a kernel consisting of just the up, down, left, and right texels. The depth texture is mainly used to generate the object outlines, while the normals texture helps with highlighting edges." A 2026 paper (Texel Splatting, Dylan Ebert) explains why this only works under orthographic projection: under perspective "no single grid snap compensates for all depths simultaneously." A Unity 6 community pipeline does the same at 640x360 internal with a 1 px outline then a sharp upscale. Sources: https://www.davidhol.land/articles/3d-pixel-art-rendering/ (n.d., excerpt) ; https://discussions.unity.com/t/recreating-t3ssel8rs-3d-pixel-art/928878 (n.d.) ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/402481025_Texel_Splatting_Perspective-Stable_3D_Pixel_Art (2026) ; https://github.com/bababuyyy/unity-isometric-pixel-pipeline (2025).

**The hobbyist 3D-to-pixel consensus (itch, Blender Artists, Unity forums, 2018 to 2025).** Render small; "without snapping, the result would look more like low res 3d objects rather than 2d sprites"; flicker appears "only when the character was under about 50 pixels tall"; "reduce the colour count of the spritesheet and clean up... editing in the end in something like Graphics Gale or Aseprite"; favour "poses that face the camera more, even though it looks jank in the 3d engine." Sources: https://discussions.unity.com/t/struggling-with-sprites-in-a-pixelated-3d-world/921716 (n.d.) ; https://hsdsz.itch.io/goblin-blade-pixel-art/comments (n.d.) ; https://blenderartists.org/t/mimicking-dead-cells-workflow-from-3d-animation-to-2d-pixel-animation/1124638 (2018) ; https://blenderartists.org/t/can-i-somehow-set-up-a-sharp-low-resolution/1323775 (n.d., "render hard pixels in Cycles by setting the pixel filter width to zero, Render > Film > Pixel Filter") ; https://blenderartists.org/t/clean-line-art-outlines-for-pixel-art/1605461 (2025, Grease Pencil line art "bled into the background," Freestyle lines "dotted and inconsistent").

**Laika and Aardman (the clay consistency tools).** Laika: characters go "pencil sketches, converted into computer generated 3d models, and later turned into maquettes," and the maquette decides "which parts will move, which will be painted, costumed"; "everything we build in the computer is informed by maquettes or sculpts or swatches of fabric"; for ParaNorman they "printed the entire paint tone book out to figure out how to calibrate" the colour printer; Coraline had 28 puppets with "45 identical pairs" of pyjamas (another source says 30), "identical down to the way the pattern broke at the seams"; the "puppet hospital" staff "go out to stages before shots to make sure the puppets match their reference models"; the ParaNorman VFX rule for CG next to clay: "if it looks too glossy or rendered, they'll address it scene by scene." Sources: http://r-wong253249-sp.blogspot.com/2014/05/ (2014, quoting Butler) ; https://www.slashfilm.com/521162/laika-3d-color-printers-create-stopmotion-animated-movie-paranorman-50-learned-set/ (2012) ; https://www.awn.com/animationworld/set-coraline-where-motion-doesnt-stop (2009) ; https://shop.laika.com/ (n.d.) ; https://www.laika.com/puppet-hospital-spotlight/ (n.d.) ; https://www.animationmagazine.net/2019/04/laikas-missing-link-around-the-world-in-stop-motion/ (2019, "the color script alone is one of our most colorful"). Aardman: Dave Alex Riddett shot Shaun the Sheep Movie and Early Man as DoP; Early Man ran "about 40" units at peak, "one camera and one animator on the set"; "all of the camera movements and lighting have to be reproducible"; for Flushed Away the CG team kept the clay read with "replacement mouths for lip-synch rather than the interpolation typically seen in computer animation" and designs "taken straight from the original plasticine models." Sources: https://vfxvoice.com/aardman-goes-back-to-stop-motion-basics-with-early-man/ (2018) ; https://en.canon-cna.com/pro/stories/stop-motion-secrets/ (n.d.) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flushed_Away (2026) ; https://www.cgw.com/Publications/CGW/2006/Volume-29-Issue-10-Oct-2006-/Flushed-with-Success.aspx (2006) ; https://theasc.com/article/wallace-gromit-vengeance-most-fowl/ (2025). The colour script itself: "an early attempt to map out the color, lighting, emotion and mood in a film," brought to Pixar by Ralph Eggleston on Toy Story. Sources: https://x.com/MatthewLuhn/status/954736252976156673 (2018) ; https://www.animationartconservation.com/design-with-a-purpose,-an-interview-with-ralph-eggleston.html (n.d.).
The transferable kit: one reference model per character that every copy is checked against before it goes on stage; a printed colour book so every material has one measured colour; one lighting setup written down so forty units match; a colour script per sequence; and for CG next to clay, a named person whose job is to say "too glossy" shot by shot. For a sprite rig those are: one master .blend per character, one palette file with ramps per material, one light in the camera rig, one colour script per district or act, and a reviewer with the glossy veto.

## SECTION A: THE TELLS OF A RENDERED SPRITE

Each tell: what it is, why it happens in a 3D-to-pixel pipeline, a measurable test, and the source. Tests assume the sprite PNG at native size with an alpha channel, plus (where named) the renderer's object-ID, material-ID, normal and depth passes for the same frame, which the rig should export anyway (Dead Cells exported a normal per frame, Holland builds outlines from depth and normals). Numbers are starting thresholds for TUNING to hold, not laws. Round-one numbers are cross-referenced; the pixel-art craft sources behind them (Pixel Joint TID 11299, Pixel Parmesan, Pixel Grimoire, Lospec, pixnote, Pixel Logic) are all cited in round one and not re-listed except where quoted.

**R1. Soft silhouette (anti-aliased edge against transparency).**
What: boundary pixels with alpha between 1 and 254, or a one-pixel fringe of colours halfway between the body and the background. Why: the renderer's pixel filter (Blender's Film Filter Size, default well above zero) and multi-sample AA average the edge; "pixel art, by definition, does not utilize anti-aliased tools" (round one tell 3). Vasseur's fix was at the source: render "without antialiasing." Test: fraction of boundary pixels (opaque pixels with a transparent 4-neighbour) whose alpha is not 255 must be 0; count of distinct colours that occur only on boundary pixels must be 0 after the palette pass. Sources: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/art-design-deep-dive-using-a-3d-pipeline-for-2d-animation-in-i-dead-cells-i- (2018) ; https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/dev/render/eevee/render_settings/film.html (2026, "lower values give more crisp renders") ; https://pixelparmesan.com/blog/anti-aliasing-fundamentals-for-pixel-artists (n.d.).

**R2. Continuous gradient shading (the ramp has no steps).**
What: across one material from light to shadow there are 8, 12, 30 colours each a few percent apart. Why: a smooth BSDF under a smooth light gives a continuous value; pixel art shows form in 3 to 5 steps per ramp and a human reads the extra steps as "uncannily smooth" (round one tell 4). Cel shading exists to "quantize conventional smooth lighting values to a small number of discrete shades" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cel_shading, 2026). Test: inside each material-ID region, sort the distinct colours by luminance; the count must be at most the ramp length N for that material (Section C sets N), and the smallest luminance gap between two adjacent ramp colours must be at least 8% of full range. A region with more than N colours or any gap under 8% is a render.

**R3. Near-duplicate colours (unquantised output).**
What: two reds that differ by one or two units in RGB, invisible at 1x, visible as a colour count of 400. Why: 8-bit output of a float render, plus Blender's output dither (Render Properties > Post Processing > Dither, which adds noise to 8-bit output when left at its default; verify the default in your version at https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/latest/render/output/properties/post_processing.html, 2026). Test: cluster all opaque colours at CIE delta E of 5; the ratio of raw colours to clusters must be exactly 1.0. Anything above 1.0 means the palette pass did not run or ran before dither was turned off. Round one tell 4's "tens of thousands of colors" is this at scale.

**R4. Soft terminator (penumbra between light and shadow).**
What: a 2 to 4 pixel transition zone where light meets shadow, filled with in-between colours. Why: area lights with a radius, soft shadow settings, and subsurface scattering (the obvious "clay" temptation) all blur the terminator; hand pixel art puts the line in one pixel and at most one anti-alias pixel on a curve. Test: along the boundary between the two highest-area ramp steps in a material region, measure the width of the band of pixels whose colour is neither step; median width must be 1 or 0, and no run of width 2 or more over 10% of the boundary length. Source for the hand rule: "the mid-tone 1px, 2px at the very most" (https://pixnote.net/en/learn/outlines/, n.d.) ; for pillow and banding from soft transitions: https://pixelparmesan.com/blog/anti-aliasing-fundamentals-for-pixel-artists (n.d.).

**R5. Uniform specular (every material shines the same).**
What: a small white hot spot of the same size and colour on cloth, skin, clay, rust and glass alike. Why: one Principled BSDF with default specular 0.5 on every object. Clay is matte; in our bible only tape, glass eyes, wet blood and chrome shine. Test: find connected components of pixels at the top colour of their material's ramp (the highlight colour) that are 3 pixels or smaller; count per material-ID; materials declared matte in the palette file must have 0; the brightest highlight on the sprite must sit on a material declared glossy. The Laika rule in one line: "if it looks too glossy or rendered, they'll address it" (https://www.slashfilm.com/521162/laika-3d-color-printers-create-stopmotion-animated-movie-paranorman-50-learned-set/, 2012).

**R6. No outline, or a dead-uniform outline.**
What: either the silhouette has no line and the body colours touch the background directly, or an edge-detect pass has drawn the same single dark colour one pixel thick all the way round, inside and out. Why: renderers do not outline; Freestyle and Grease Pencil "bled into the background" and came out "dotted and inconsistent" (https://blenderartists.org/t/clean-line-art-outlines-for-pixel-art/1605461, 2025); a screen-space edge pass gives one colour. Pixel art uses selective outlining: "where the sprite is darkest, we'll use a dark color for the outline, and where it's lightest we'll use a lighter color" (https://lospec.com/articles/pixel-art-outlines-part-2-using-color, n.d.); Derek Yu's basics: "replacing a lot of the black outline with lighter colors" (http://derekyu.com/makegames/pixelart.html, n.d.). Test: (a) a closed 1-px ring of pixels exists around the silhouette whose luminance is lower than the adjacent body pixel on the shadow side; (b) the ring uses between 2 and 4 distinct colours; (c) the ring's mean luminance on the light-side half is higher than on the shadow-side half by at least 10% of range; (d) no 2x2 block is entirely outline (round one tell 12, "doubles"). One colour all round, or no ring, fails.

**R7. Pillow shading from camera-axis light or heavy fill.**
What: the lightest colour sits at the centre of each form and the shadow rings it evenly. Why: a key light near the camera axis, or world ambient and fill lights nearly as strong as the key, flatten the light direction (round one tell 13 covers the hand-drawn version). Test: per material region, the centroid of the top ramp step minus the centroid of the bottom ramp step is a vector; its length must be at least 15% of the sprite height and its angle within 30 degrees of the declared screen light (round one gate 3). Source for the defect: "pillow shading as flat concentric rings with no light direction" (https://www.pixel-editor.com/articles/pixel-art-dithering, n.d.).

**R8. Banding (stacked parallel steps that follow the geometry).**
What: along a curved edge the ramp steps stack as equal-length parallel staircases, so the grid shows and the curve goes flat. Why: a smooth normal quantised by a ramp produces iso-lines that hug the silhouette; Pixel Parmesan: banding "occurs when lines of the same length are placed parallel to each other" and "makes the grid visible" (https://pixelparmesan.com/blog/anti-aliasing-fundamentals-for-pixel-artists, n.d.); Pixel Joint calls the outline-hugging version "hugging" (https://pixeljoint.com/forum/forum_posts.asp?TID=11299, n.d.). Test: for every pair of adjacent ramp-step boundaries, compute the run lengths of their horizontal steps; a run of 3 or more steps where the two boundaries have identical run lengths at the same x positions is a band; more than 2 bands per sprite is a tell.

**R9. Orphan pixels and speckle from the downscale.**
What: single pixels of a colour that touches no other pixel of that colour, scattered on the interior; or a stipple on a surface that was meant to be clay texture. Why: nearest-neighbour sampling of a textured or bumpy surface picks random texels; Ion Fury: "downscaling the large renders to the target resolution causes many small details to disappear," which is the same sampling turned the other way. Pixel craft: "orphan pixels, those one-off dots floating in space, usually look like noise or accidental specks" (https://www.the-pixel-artist.com/articles/top-ten-things-new-pixel-artists-should-know, n.d.). Test: count 4-connected components of size 1 or 2 that are not on the outline ring and not on a whitelist of glint coordinates; more than 1 per 400 opaque pixels is a tell. Tools exist for the fix (Adopt Pixels, Aseprite extension, https://astropulse.itch.io, n.d.).

**R10. Jaggies on the silhouette (non-monotonic staircases).**
What: a diagonal or curve whose step lengths go 3, 1, 2, 1, 3 instead of 3, 2, 2, 1, 1. Why: a smooth curve sampled at the grid without an artist deciding where the steps go; "they're a common result of using nearest-neighbor interpolation" (https://alexharri.com/blog/ascii-rendering, n.d.). Round one tell 10 has the hand version. Test: trace the outline; split into monotone segments; within each segment the sequence of step lengths must be non-increasing or non-decreasing; count violations per 100 outline pixels; over 5 is a tell. Ion Fury's "crisper, more pixel art look" pass is this by hand (https://archive.3drealms.com/devblog/dev-blog-2-art-ion-maiden/, 2018).

**R11. Thin-part dropout (parts that vanish and return).**
What: a strap, antenna, whisker, wire or tape end that is under one pixel wide in the model shows in some frames and disappears in others. Why: sub-pixel geometry sampled by one sample per pixel; the forum observation that flicker shows "only when the character was under about 50 pixels tall" is this (https://discussions.unity.com/t/struggling-with-sprites-in-a-pixelated-3d-world/921716, n.d.). Test: with the object-ID pass, count pixels per part per frame; a part whose count is zero in frame k and nonzero in frames k minus 1 and k plus 1 is a dropout; zero allowed. Source fix: Dead Cells kept the model's parts chunky because it was only 50 px tall (Vasseur, 2018).

**R12. Pixel creep (features drift between frames on a hold).**
What: in an idle or hold, pixels along edges change colour frame to frame although the part is not meant to move; the sprite "boils." Why: the model's position in world units does not sit on the pixel grid, so a tiny motion crosses a pixel boundary somewhere each frame. Holland's fix: snap the camera (for us, the model root) to a texel-sized grid (https://www.davidhol.land/articles/3d-pixel-art-rendering/, n.d.). Test: on a declared hold (the idle's rest keys), XOR each frame against the previous; changed pixels outside the declared moving mask must be under 3% of opaque pixels.

**R13. Perfect symmetry.**
What: the front-facing sprite's left half is the mirror of its right half, pixel for pixel. Why: the model was built with a Mirror modifier and posed straight on; "a fully mirrored character reads as a chess piece" (https://neta.art/blog/16x16-pixel-art-rules, n.d.); the fix is "one asymmetric protrusion, such as a shoulder pad, a backpack, a horn, a lantern on the hip" and a stance change: "rotate the shoulders slightly, shift the weight, and angle the head" (https://www.sprite-ai.art/blog/anime-pixel-art, n.d.). Test: mirror the front-facing silhouette mask about its vertical centroid axis; intersection over union above 0.97 is a tell; also colour agreement of mirrored opaque pixels above 95% is a tell. Note the lighting trap on mirroring: if a facing is produced by flipping, "mirroring moves the lit side to the right and the sprite appears to flicker between frames" (same source); facings are rendered, never flipped (see R20).

**R14. No silhouette exaggeration (correct proportions, mannequin read).**
What: a figure whose head is 1/7 of its height, hands smaller than its face, weapon thin as a pencil, at 48 to 100 pixels tall. Why: the modeller built an anatomically right body; at sprite size right is wrong. Dead Cells drew a 2D model sheet first, then built the 3D to match it; Ion Fury "focus on overall form." Test: from the silhouette, head height (the top blob above the shoulder narrowing) divided by total height at least 0.22 for a humanoid under 64 px; hand blob width at least 0.8 of head width; held weapon at least 2 px thick at its thinnest; 8x8 downsampled silhouette not a filled rectangle and convex-hull solidity under 0.85 (round one tell 17). These ratios go in the style sheet and TUNING holds them.

**R15. Uniform detail density (texture everywhere).**
What: every surface of the sprite carries the same high-frequency detail: fingerprints on the clay, grain on the tape, scratches on the metal, all at once, all over. Why: the texture artist textured everything and the render shows everything (round one tell 16). Hyper Light Drifter's rule: "big sections of flat colour then small details etched on top" (https://medium.com/the-space-ape-games-experience/hyper-light-drifter-ui-breakdown-c2d9cfe0a192, n.d.). Test: round one gate 5 applied per sprite: edge density per 8x8 block, Gini over 0.4, quietest quartile under 40% of the busiest, and the declared hero block (the face or the tape seam) in the top 10%.

**R16. Mid-grey everything (no three-value read).**
What: in greyscale the whole sprite sits between 35% and 65% luminance; nothing is dark, nothing is light. Why: physically based materials under a balanced light rarely hit either end; a pixel artist pushes the shadow ramp down and the highlight up. Test: round one gate 7: three luminance bins (under 30%, 30 to 65%, over 65%) each owning at least 12% of the opaque pixels.

**R17. Studio three-point lighting (two highlights and a rim).**
What: a bright edge on the shadow side (rim light), a second soft highlight (fill), a sheen on the floor side. Why: the 3D habit of key, fill and rim; a sprite has one light. Test: no pixel at the material's top ramp colour within 1 px of the silhouette on the half of the sprite facing away from the declared light; at most one highlight lobe per material region (one connected component of the top ramp step larger than 3 px). A deliberate backlight for a boss is declared in the sheet and whitelisted.

**R18. Hue-constant ramps (shadow is only darker).**
What: the shadow colour is the base colour times 0.6; same hue, same or more saturation. Why: a renderer multiplies albedo by light; a pixel artist shifts hue toward blue or purple in shadow and toward yellow in light (round one tell 6). Test: for every ramp in the palette file, hue delta from darkest to lightest at least 8 degrees and saturation not rising into shadow; and for the sprite, every pixel in the palette (so the test lives in the palette, not the render). Source: "shadows shifted toward blue and highlights toward yellow" is the common guide rule (https://spritelab.dev/guides/pixel-art-palettes, n.d.).

**R19. Floating feet and a renderer shadow blob.**
What: the bottom opaque row wanders between facings and frames; a soft grey ellipse with 20 alpha levels under the feet. Why: no contact snapping, and a shadow catcher plane in the render. Test: the lowest opaque row of the foot parts (object-ID) is the same row across the 8 facings and across all frames of a ground-locked animation, plus or minus 1; any pixel with alpha between 1 and 254 fails R1 anyway; a shadow, if present, is its own sprite at one flat colour. Source: Octopath puts the shadow in the scene, not in the sprite (Unreal spotlight, 2018).

**R20. Facings that disagree (height, pivot, light side, turntable light).**
What: the eight facings have bounding boxes of different heights, pivots in different pixels, or the highlight swings from the left side to the right side as the character turns. Why: the camera orbited the model but the light stayed in world space, so the light rotated relative to the screen; or the model was rotated and the light came with it. For a sprite the light is fixed to the SCREEN, upper-left, in every facing. Diablo II rendered eight angles through one tool; the consistency came from the tool, not from eight artists (Game Developer postmortem, 2000). Test: bounding-box height across 8 facings within plus or minus 1 px; pivot pixel identical; highlight-centroid-minus-shadow-centroid angle within 30 degrees of the declared screen light in all 8 (R7 run per facing); silhouette area across facings within 25% of the mean.

**R21. Interior edges with no line (parts melt together).**
What: arm against torso, strap against chest, jaw against neck, with no darker or lighter 1-px line between them, only a soft value change. Why: the renderer separates parts by depth and normals, which the quantised colour image loses; hand pixel art draws the interior line, often lighter than the silhouette line because "there's no background to worry about, so you can go as light as you want for interior lines" (https://lospec.com/articles/pixel-art-outlines-part-2-using-color, n.d.). Test: for every boundary between two object-ID regions of the same material, at least 70% of its length must have a 1-px pixel on one side whose colour is a different ramp step from both neighbours.

**R22. The lighting is smoother than the sprite (runtime light mixels).**
What: a normal-mapped sprite lit at runtime shows a smooth lamp falloff across a 4-colour body, or a glow at full screen resolution on a 4x pixel. Why: Dead Cells' runtime toon light reads to some as "overdone" (Hacker News, 2018); Sea of Stars is praised because its light effects "render at the same pixel-scale resolution" (Quarter to Three, 2023). Test (on a screenshot, not the sprite): every lit pixel of the character is one of the palette colours, and the lighting's pixel size equals the sprite's native pixel size (round one gate 1 run on the lit character region). This is the tell that decides baked light versus runtime light for us: baked light passes by construction.

## SECTION B: THE HAND-FINISH PASS, STEP BY STEP

What Ion Fury describes, what Dead Cells avoided by source settings, what Diablo II and Donkey Kong Country did with palette tools, and what the pixel craft books add, folded into one order. The order matters: palette before outline, outline before selout, cleanup after everything that creates colours, and never anti-alias after quantising. Each step says SCRIPT (a program can do it every time), SCRIPT THEN EYE (a program does it and flags, a person confirms) or EYE (a person must decide).

**B0. Export the passes, not just the picture. SCRIPT.**
From the rig, per frame per facing: the colour image (already stepped by the toon ramp), the alpha, the object-ID pass (one integer per part: head, torso, arm L, tape, eyes), the material-ID pass, the normal pass and the depth pass. Dead Cells shipped colour plus normal per frame (Vasseur, 2018); Holland's outlines need depth and normal (davidhol.land, n.d.). Every gate in Section D reads these; the eye pass uses the ID pass as a selection mask in Aseprite.

**B1. Lock the pixel size. SCRIPT.**
If rendered at native size, nothing to do. If rendered at 2x or 4x (Section C says when), downscale once with nearest neighbour or with a k-centroid downscaler (K-Centroid Downscale, Aseprite extension, https://astropulse.itch.io, n.d.), never bilinear, never in two passes with percentages. One forum thread's stepwise-percentage advice (https://itch.io/post/13916320, n.d.) is for recovering damaged art and does not apply to a clean render.

**B2. Harden the alpha. SCRIPT.**
Threshold alpha at 50%: 255 above, 0 below. Then remove colour fringing by replacing every boundary pixel's colour with the nearest palette colour of its material (from the material-ID pass), not with the nearest colour in RGB, which pulls the background in. This kills R1 and R19's blob at once.

**B3. Quantise to the palette, per material, no dithering. SCRIPT.**
For every material-ID region, map every pixel to the nearest step of THAT material's ramp in the master palette file; a skin pixel may not borrow a tape colour because it happened to be closer in RGB. Ramp lengths N per material are set in the sheet (Section C proposes 4 for clay, 3 for tape, 3 for metal, 2 for glass eyes plus one glint). Dithering off: Aseprite's RGB-to-Indexed conversion exposes ordered, old, error-diffusion and none (https://community.aseprite.org/t/what-are-the-changepixelformat-dithering-modes/8385, n.d.; CLI flags at https://www.aseprite.org/docs/cli/, n.d.); choose none. Aseprite users warn the built-in palette match is weak and "depends heavily on how close the palette colors are to the image" (https://community.aseprite.org/t/dithering-palletize-automation-help/19431, n.d.), which is one more reason to do this step as our own script against the ID pass, and to set the ramp colours in the toon shader so the render is already in palette (Section C) and this step is a check that changes nothing. PixelOver offers "palette indexing and dithering" and "pixel-perfect outlines" on imported OBJ renders (https://pixelover.io/, n.d.; https://store.steampowered.com/app/1762550/PixelOver/, 2021) and is a fine second opinion, not the pipeline.

**B4. Merge sliver bands. SCRIPT THEN EYE.**
After quantising, any ramp-step band thinner than 2 px sandwiched between two other steps is merged into the larger neighbour. This kills R4 and most of R8. The eye then looks at where the terminator landed on the hero frames: on a face it should run down the nose and under the cheek, not across the eye. Moving it means moving the light or the ramp stop in the rig, not painting it, so every facing moves together.

**B5. Remove orphans and speckle. SCRIPT THEN EYE.**
Delete 1-px and 2-px islands not on the outline (Adopt Pixels does this; a 20-line script does this). The eye re-adds the ones that were deliberate: eye glints, a tape edge catching light, a single tooth. Those coordinates go on the whitelist so the gate stops flagging them.

**B6. Build the outline from the passes, then colour it. SCRIPT THEN EYE.**
Silhouette outline: 1 px ring where alpha meets transparency, from a 4-neighbour kernel (Holland's up, down, left, right kernel keeps it one pixel). Interior lines: 1 px where object-ID changes and depth steps by more than a threshold. Colour the ring by selective outlining: on the shadow side the darkest step of the adjacent material's ramp (or the palette's one shared dark); on the light side the adjacent material's mid step; pixnote's limit, "use sel-out sparingly, only on long diagonals/curves, keeping the mid-tone 1px, 2px at the very most" and "first wrap the whole silhouette in a dark outline to lock the shape" (https://pixnote.net/en/learn/outlines/, n.d.). Interior lines lighter than the silhouette line (Lospec, n.d.). The eye fixes the places where the pass drew a line that the drawing does not want (a tape fold that should read as one shape) and the places where two parts in the same material need a line the pass could not see.

**B7. Fix the jaggies on the silhouette. EYE, with a SCRIPT flag.**
The script marks non-monotonic staircases (R10). The eye decides the step order, one pixel at a time, on the hero frames and on the first frame of every beast's idle. This is exactly the Ion Fury step: "cleans them up for a crisper, more pixel art look" (2018). It is the most expensive step and the one that most separates a sprite from a render, so budget it (B11).

**B8. Place the highlights. EYE.**
Delete whatever specular the render left (R5 should have been off at the source). Then put 1 to 3 clusters of the top ramp step where the form turns toward the upper-left light: the brow, the shoulder, the knuckle, the tape's bright edge. Clay stays matte; tape, glass eyes, wet blood and chrome get one hot pixel each. The clay rule from Laika's VFX team is the reviewer's veto in one word: glossy.

**B9. Restore the details the downscale ate. EYE.**
Ion Fury: "restores lost details, polishes the sprites and adds stylisation" (2018). For us the list is short and fixed by the bible: the eyes (two pixels, always readable, the analog-horror stare), the mouth seam, the tape seams, the one asymmetric prop (R13), the weapon's edge, the faction colour patch (COLOUR IS TERRITORY). Nothing else is restored; "big sections of flat colour then small details etched on top" (Hyper Light Drifter breakdown, n.d.).

**B10. Push the silhouette in the MODEL, not the sprite. EYE decides, SCRIPT re-renders.**
If the figure fails R14, do not paint a bigger head on 8 facings times 30 frames. Scale the head, hands and weapon in the .blend, re-render everything, re-run B1 to B9. This is the whole point of Vasseur's pipeline: "changes to a weapon's speed were quick to make" and timing fixed "by moving keyframes on the timeline" (2018). The 2D model sheet he drew before modelling is the place the exaggeration is decided; we have the robot and the truck as the sheet he already approved.

**B11. Budget the eye. The rule.**
Dead Cells: one artist, keys by hand, in-betweens by the tool, 30 fps (2018). Ion Fury: the artist "picks specific animation frames" to overpaint (2018). Blasphemous: every frame by hand at 360 high, and a team to do it (2017 to 2023). Our budget rule, proposed: the player character and the three generations' hero portraits get the full B4 to B9 eye pass on every frame and every facing; the 60 bosses get it on the idle's rest key, the attack's contact key, the hurt key and the death key, 8 facings each; everything else (champions, the ~75 enemy types, 17 beasts, props, vehicles) gets scripts only (B1 to B6) plus a five-minute eye check on the contact sheet, and the gate list catches the rest. A boss that fails the eye check gets its model fixed (B10), not its frames painted.

**B12. Check the eight facings and all frames together. SCRIPT.**
Section D's gates on the contact sheet: heights, pivots, light side, hold stability, dropouts. Onion-skin the facings in Aseprite for the eye on heroes only.

**Which steps are the hand.** The honest answer: B7, B8, B9 and the decision in B10. Everything else is a script that must run every render, and a script that runs every render is also a gate. Dead Cells proves that with the right source settings (Section C) the scripted steps are nearly no-ops and the sprite is already 90% there; Ion Fury proves the last 10% is a person with a pencil tool on chosen frames; Donkey Kong Country proves that the palette step is the one that cannot be skipped.

## SECTION C: THE RIG SETTINGS THAT PREVENT THE TELLS AT THE SOURCE

Settings for the headless Blender rig, one .blend per character, one Python render script shared by all. The script IS the law here (A LAW WITHOUT A MACHINE GATE IS NOT ENFORCED): a gate reads the script and the .blend and refuses a render whose settings drift. Blender's command line: `-b` runs without UI "often used for UI-less rendering," `-f` renders one frame, `-a` the range, `-o` sets the output path with `#` padding, `-E` picks the engine, and arguments execute in order so output path comes before the render flag (https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/latest/advanced/command_line/arguments.html, 2026). Each item below names the tell it prevents.

**C1. Orthographic camera, fixed pitch, eight yaws.** Camera type Orthographic (every tutorial and Holland agree; perspective makes snapping impossible, Texel Splatting 2026). Pitch is the project's declared angle from the 45 DEGREE ART LAW, written once in the script. The eight facings are the MODEL rotated in 45-degree steps about its vertical axis while camera and light stay still, so the light stays on the screen's upper-left in every facing (prevents R20). Facings are rendered, never flipped (R13's lighting trap).

**C2. Pixel-perfect orthographic scale.** Orthographic Scale "sets the amount of Blender Units the widest field of view corresponds to" (https://steamcommunity.com/app/365670/discussions/0/350542683187627960/, n.d.). Pick a pixels-per-unit (ppu) for the whole game, then orthographic_scale = render_width_px / ppu, so one pixel = 1/ppu units; a devtalk thread puts the same relation as 100 px per metre giving a 1 m plane exactly 100 px (https://devtalk.blender.org/t/pre-rendered-graphics-game-dev-wanting-to-add-a-setting-to-anchor-orthographic-scale-to-render-resolution/38721, n.d.). Verify once by rendering a 1-unit cube face-on and counting ppu pixels; the gate renders this calibration cube every run. Output percentage 100% (a tutorial notes it defaults to 50% in some templates; https://www.javin-inc.com/blenderpixel/, n.d.). Render resolution = the sprite's native box (the project's 112 px box for a person; the ENVELOPE and GROUND MAY ZOOM laws already fix "one pixel size"). For an isometric-style pitch a half-pixel Y shift of 1 / height * 0.5 keeps edges on the grid (https://clintbellanger.net/articles/isometric_tiles/, n.d.). Prevents round one tells 1 and 2 and R19.

**C3. Snap the model root to the pixel grid every frame.** Before each frame renders, round the root bone's screen-space position to a multiple of 1/ppu (Holland's camera snap applied to the subject, davidhol.land n.d.). The pivot pixel is written in the sprite's metadata so the engine draws every facing and frame about the same pixel. Prevents R12 and R20.

**C4. One light, fixed to the camera, no fill, no rim, no ambient.** One Sun lamp parented to the camera, from the screen's upper-left: azimuth 30 to 45 degrees left of the camera axis, elevation 45 degrees, written in the sheet as the declared light. Angle 0 (a point source, no soft shadow radius). World colour black or the darkest palette colour with strength 0; Ambient Occlusion off; no other lamps. Shadows on, hard. Prevents R4, R7, R17 and R20. Dead Cells lit at runtime instead; we bake because our fight board is a fixed-angle house tile, the light is constant on screen, and baked light passes R22 by construction. Export the normal pass anyway (B0) so a runtime light can be added later for a flashlight, a muzzle flash or a torch without redrawing.

**C5. Flat stepped shading: Shader to RGB into a Constant Color Ramp with the palette's exact colours.** EEVEE (Shader to RGB "is an Eevee feature; it doesn't work in Cycles," https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/how-to-get-anime-toon-look-blender, 2026). Per material: Diffuse BSDF, Shader to RGB, Map Range to spread the value (one guide notes the stops otherwise "bunch up on the left," https://github.com/TehMerow/Tutorials/wiki/Toon-Shading-with-Eevee, n.d.), then a Color Ramp set to Constant interpolation ("sharp shading edges between each color," https://artisticrender.com/cel-shading-in-blender/, n.d.; the manual's Constant mode, https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/latest/interface/controls/templates/color_ramp.html, 2026). The ramp's stops are not a tint multiplied over a base: each stop is set to the exact hex of a palette ramp step, so the render leaves the lamp already in palette with hue shift built in (prevents R2, R3, R18). Proposed N per material for the clay-and-tape bible, for TUNING to hold: clay 4 steps (shadow, mid, light, highlight), tape 3, painted metal 3, rubber 3, glass eyes 2 plus one glint pixel, chrome and blood 2 with a glint. Specular: none on any material; glints are placed by hand in B8 (prevents R5). Subsurface, sheen, clearcoat, bump maps and image textures of surface detail: off; clay reads as clay by its silhouette and its one asymmetric fingerprint shape, not by a bump map that becomes speckle (prevents R9, R15). An emission-based variant (Emission mixed with a ramp) also pixelates cleanly (https://savedpixel.com/blog/blender-pixel-art, n.d.).

**C6. No anti-aliasing anywhere.** EEVEE Render Samples 1 and Film Filter Size at its minimum (0.01 px; the manual: "lower values give more crisp renders," https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/dev/render/eevee/render_settings/film.html, 2026; a Blender 3.3 guide uses 0.01 px for the same reason, https://styly.cc/tips/blender-lowpoly-nyu/, 2022; in Cycles the same control is Pixel Filter width, https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/latest/render/cycles/render_settings/film.html, 2026). Motion blur off, depth of field off, bloom off, screen-space reflections off, soft shadows off, volumetrics off. Film Transparent on for alpha. Prevents R1 and R4. The gate reads these values from the script.

**C7. Colour management that does not touch the palette.** View Transform Standard (not Filmic, not AgX), Look None, Exposure 0, Gamma 1, Sequencer colour space sRGB. Otherwise the exact hex set in C5 comes out as a different hex. Prevents R3.

**C8. Dither off, 8-bit PNG RGBA.** Render Properties > Post Processing > Dither set to 0 (it adds noise to 8-bit output when above 0; verify the default in the installed version, https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/latest/render/output/properties/post_processing.html, 2026). Output PNG, RGBA, 8-bit, compression any (lossless). Prevents R3 and R9. The in-sprite dithering question is closed the same way: none in the ramp, none in the compositor, none in the Aseprite conversion (B3). Analog-horror grain, if it ever comes, is a screen layer the UI and DIRECTION lanes own, never baked into a sprite.

**C9. Palette quantisation order.** (1) render already in palette (C5); (2) downscale if rendered at a multiple (B1); (3) alpha threshold (B2); (4) quantise per material as a check (B3); (5) sliver merge and orphans (B4, B5); (6) outline then selout (B6); (7) hand pass (B7 to B9); (8) gates. Never: AA or blur after step 3; outline before quantise (the outline colours would be re-mapped); dither at any step.

**C10. Native render versus 2x and downscale.** Native (render resolution = sprite box) when the toon ramp supplies the steps and the model has no part thinner than one pixel at ppu; this is the Dead Cells route ("render the mesh in a very small size and without antialiasing," 2018) and it gives the eye pass a sprite that is already a sprite. Render at 2x (or 4x) and downscale with k-centroid when the model has thin parts that drop out at native (R11) or when a prop needs its silhouette steps chosen by the downscaler rather than by a person; the downscale votes per 2x2 block and the thin part survives as one pixel in every frame. Rule: heroes and bosses native (an eye will touch them anyway, and native keeps the staircases decidable); props, vehicles and far beasts at 2x plus k-centroid. Never the compositor Pixelate node for a final sprite (Blender Studio 2024: it "included anti-aliasing"); if the compositor is used at all, a Scale node with Nearest filtering (https://savedpixel.com/blog/blender-pixel-art, n.d.).

**C11. Passes and metadata.** Enable Object Index, Material Index, Normal and Z (depth) passes; write them beside the colour PNG with the same name stem. Write a JSON per sprite: ppu, pixel box, pivot pixel, declared light angle, palette file hash, material N table, whitelist of deliberate orphans, hero or non-hero. Every gate in Section D reads this JSON; no number lives in a gate.

**C12. Frame counts belong to ANIMATION under the 120 BPM and ENVELOPE laws.** Dead Cells ran 30 fps and keyed like 2D (2018). Our body is drawn at twelve keys a bar and nothing between two keys is drawn (AN ENVELOPE RAMPS SLOWER THAN THE GRID, 9/24), so the rig renders exactly the keys the law names and no in-betweens; this also makes the eye pass twelve frames a bar, not thirty.

**C13. The model sheet comes first.** Vasseur: 2D model sheet, then the 3D (2018). Laika: sketch, CG model, maquette, and "everything we build in the computer is informed by maquettes" (2014). For us the robot and the truck are the maquettes he approved; every new character gets a one-page sheet (front, side, back at native size, the asymmetric prop, the palette ramps it uses, the glossy parts list) that DIRECTION signs before the .blend starts.

## SECTION D: TEN THINGS A GATE COULD CHECK ON A RENDERED SPRITE

Ordered by how much of the "render" read each one explains, weighted by how cheap it is. Numbers 2 to 10 run on the PNG plus the ID passes and JSON from C11. Number 1 runs on the script and .blend before any render and is the cheapest check on the list. Round one's ten gates still apply and are not repeated; these are the render-specific additions.

1. **Source settings (C1 to C8).** Read the render script and the .blend: camera ORTHO, orthographic_scale = width / ppu, render samples 1, filter size at most 0.01, dither 0, view transform Standard, film transparent true, motion blur and DoF and bloom and AO false, exactly one lamp of type Sun with angle 0 and parented to the camera, every material's ramp interpolation Constant with every stop colour present in the palette file, specular 0 on every material. One drift fails the render before it starts. This gate is a text diff and prevents R1 to R5, R7, R17, R18 and R20 at once.
2. **Binary alpha and clean boundary (R1).** Zero pixels with alpha in 1 to 254; zero colours that occur only on boundary pixels.
3. **Per-material colour count and ramp gaps (R2, R3).** For each material-ID region, distinct colours at most N from the JSON table, every colour in the palette file, delta E 5 cluster ratio exactly 1.0, adjacent ramp luminance gap at least 8%.
4. **Terminator and band width (R4, R8).** No sandwiched band thinner than 2 px over more than 10% of a boundary; at most 2 identical-run-length stacks per sprite.
5. **Orphans off the whitelist (R9).** 1-px and 2-px islands not on the outline and not in the JSON whitelist: at most 1 per 400 opaque pixels; 0 for heroes.
6. **Outline ring and selout (R6, R21).** A closed 1-px ring; no 2x2 outline block; 2 to 4 ring colours; light-half ring luminance at least 10% above shadow-half; at least 70% of same-material part boundaries carry a 1-px line.
7. **One light, from the screen's upper-left, in all eight facings (R7, R17, R20).** Highlight-minus-shadow centroid vector at least 15% of height and within 30 degrees of the declared angle, per facing; no top-ramp pixel within 1 px of the silhouette on the away-from-light half.
8. **Eight facings agree (R19, R20).** Bounding-box height within plus or minus 1 px; pivot pixel identical; foot row identical within 1; silhouette area within 25% of the mean across facings.
9. **Hold stability and no dropouts (R11, R12).** On declared holds, changed pixels outside the moving mask under 3%; no object-ID part with zero pixels in a frame between two frames where it has pixels.
10. **Symmetry and silhouette push (R13, R14).** Front-facing mirror IoU under 0.97; head ratio, hand ratio, weapon thickness and convex-hull solidity against the sheet's numbers; 8x8 silhouette not a filled rectangle.

What a gate still cannot check and a reviewer must: whether the terminator falls where the face wants it (B4), whether the three highlights are on the right three things (B8), whether the one restored detail is the one the bible names (B9), and the Laika word, glossy. The gates buy the reviewer's time for those four.

## SECTION E: ONE PARAGRAPH FOR THE ART DIRECTOR, NEXT WEEK

Build the rig before any new character: one Python render script with every Section C setting hard-coded and a gate that diffs it (D1), one palette file with the clay, tape, metal, rubber, eye, chrome and blood ramps at the proposed N and the hue shift already in the hex, one camera-parented Sun at the declared angle, and a calibration cube that renders every run. Then put the robot and the truck, the two things he loved, through it: eight facings and the idle's rest key each, native for the robot, 2x plus k-centroid for the truck, and run the ten checks on the raw output to learn which tells the source settings already kill and which survive. Hand-finish the robot's eight rest frames yourself with B4 to B9 (the sliver merge, the orphans, the selout, the staircases, three highlights, the eyes and the tape seams) and register raw-next-to-finished in the VOTE tab, so what he votes on is the exact size of the hand pass, not a theory of it. Write the one-page clay-and-tape sheet while the renders run: the light angle, the N table, the matte rule with the four glossy exceptions, the asymmetric-prop rule, the head-hand-weapon ratios, and the hero budget of B11, and hand the numbers to TUNING and the ten checks to PLUMBER. Do not start a third character until the robot passes D1 to D10 green and he has thumbed the finished eight, because every setting that changes after the third character is eight facings times every frame of everything redrawn, and Vasseur's whole lesson is that the rig is where that cost is paid once.
